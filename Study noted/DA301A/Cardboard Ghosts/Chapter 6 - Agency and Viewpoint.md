## A Thing That Happens To You

A key component of the question of player identities and how they are expressed is this: are they acting, or being acted upon? Most games, particularly wargames, prefer player roles in the former camp. It’s the fantasy promised on all those old Avalon Hill boxes: *Now YOU Are In Command*. Such games provide a historically accurate model, if by “accuracy” you mean “events happen the way they did historically”.

But this is not an accurate model of history as it is actually experienced while living through it, of a complex web of mostly unpredictable accidents and intentions only making sense in hindsight, of people trying to survive and thrive in an environment where their control over their circumstances is often limited. Most people living through history do not command armies or shape the destiny of a nation (and those who do often have far less control than we ascribe to them).

Over the last decade, a number of games have at least gotten us closer to the circumstances of ordinary lives by positioning the player as people merely *adjacent* to power. We’ve already discussed Simonsson’s *Turncoats*, in which we play not as the noble houses pursuing their monarchal ambitions, but as lesser houses seeking their favor, influence, and power, nudging events through our support and duplicity.

## Pax Porfiriana and Pax Pamir

Simonsson is an admirer of *Pax Pamir*, the second game in the *Pax* series, an informal family of games in which, broadly speaking, players are interlopers and middlemen navigating times of political crisis. The first of these, *Pax Porfiriana*, was designed by Matt Eklund, Jim Gutt, and Phil Eklund, based on the latter’s *Lords of the Sierra Madre*. Set during the Porfiriato – the three decade dictatorship of Porfirio Díaz – players are wealthy businessmen accumulating four kinds of points: Loyalty, Outrage, Command, and Revolution. Each corresponds to a kind of “Topple” card, and if a player has enough of those points when that specific card is activated, they win the game. ^p54

For example, if the Topple cares about one’s loyalty to the regime, and you have enough Loyalty, the dying Diaz names you as his successor. Of course, ensuring you have enough points of a given type when that card happens to come up *and* is eligible to be activated can be tricky, especially as you have no idea which Topple is coming up next. Players must constantly shift their positions, responding to the opportunities presented by the game’s large and chaotic card market.[^1]

*Pax Pamir* positions its players as local leaders in Afghanistan during “The Great Game”, a nineteenth-century proto-cold war between the Russian and British Empires. As in *Turncoats*, you want to be in good with whoever comes out on top, be it one of these foreign empires or a native element wishing to be free of their influence.

Loyalty to one of these factions is declared openly, and limits the opportunities that are available to you. If, for example, you are aligned with the British, you can buy and play red British cards, but cannot use yellow Russian or green Afghan cards. You can change your loyalty, but in doing so, you lose everything you gained from your previous master. And so, you’re only going to abandon them when they are clearly losing, or when it’s clear that they, the faction, will win, but you, the player, will not.

The Topple cards of *Pax Porfiriana* are replaced here with Dominance cards. Instead of checking if you have the right kinds of points at the right time, four times during the game you check to see if one of the three factions has a dominant board position. If they do, those players score points for their influence within that faction; if they don’t, players score points for their own power and influence on the board.

Because of this, the identities you inhabit are ambitious but not grasping. In *Pax Porfiriana*, the prize is succession – each of you striving to become Mexico’s next President-for-life. But the winning player in *Pax Pamir* doesn’t necessarily become the next emir of Afghanistan. There is no tangible prize specified at all. Instead, you are trying to do the best for yourself, your court, and your people. ^p55

The victory points the game awards you are a measure of how well you navigated these tumultuous times, your ability to place a bet on the winning side, and your ability to maintain your own local political power and influence in case that bet doesn’t pay off. You are the people caught in the middle of this thing, trying to make the best of it, but you are also manipulating these circumstances and these empires in pursuit of your own agenda. The players are both catspaw and cat.

Your agency is limited both by the faction to which you declare your loyalty, and by the unpredictable flow of cards through the game’s market. Only half of the game’s 142 cards are used at a full table of five players, which means you can’t plan on certain cards showing up or being absent. One game might have more British cards in the deck than Russian or Afghan, which might naturally swing the overall game in that faction’s favor.

These cards are purchased using rupees, which are placed on cheaper cards within the market; when someone purchases one of those cards, they gain the rupees on it. New rupees are seldom introduced to the game, resulting in a mostly closed economy. This, too, narrows your number of viable options. Once you’ve purchased a card, you’ll want to play it on a subsequent action. But the number of cards you can have in your tableau – “in your court” – is limited to three plus the number of purple symbols within that court. All these elements make it difficult to exert control over your own fate. To do the things you want to do, you must work within those restrictions, exercising agency in an environment which conspires to deny it to you.

And in a nutshell, isn’t that what living through history – living through *now* – feels like for most people? Isn’t that more immersive than imagining ourselves the heads of armies and empires? By asking us to assume these identities, the game aligns our sympathies with them. This makes the subject more immediate and human, aided by cards which carefully center the rich cultural history of Afghanistan. The framing provided by this choice of role makes an anti-imperialist argument.

It is interesting to compare the second edition of *Pax Pamir* to *The Great Game: Rival Empires in Central Asia 1837–1886*, a board game designed by John Gorkowski[^2] released around the same time. This game places its two players squarely in the roles of Britain and Russia, moving their pieces across what the game’s ad copy calls “a chessboard of Central Asia.” This embodies the grotesque way those empires saw the region and their inhabitants. We are asked to inhabit the viewpoint and prejudices of these superpowers, to do the sorts of things superpowers do to other countries, and to regard our actions with the same horrific detachment. That’s not necessarily a bad approach, nor is it “less moral” than *Pax Pamir* (though I do think *Pamir* is a more interesting and noteworthy game). ^p56

## Viewpoint and Horror in Meltwater

*The Great Game* is rather like *Twilight Struggle*, which likewise sees two superpowers treating everywhere else as chess squares and everyone else as pawns, and likewise trusts us to recognize the innate horror of this dehumanizing worldview. As *Twilight Struggle* designers Jason Matthews and Ananda Gupta explains, their game

> basically accepts all of the internal logic of the Cold War as true – even those parts of it that are demonstrably false. Therefore, the only relationships that matter in this game are those between a nation and the superpowers. The world provides a convenient chess board for US and Soviet ambitions, but all other nations are mere pawns … Furthermore, not only does the domino theory work, it is a prerequisite for extending influence into a region. Historians would rightly dispute all of these assumptions[.][^3]

Which is not to handwave away critiques of this approach. It is far more common to be asked to play as the oppressor than as the oppressed, and often this is done without recognition of the fact we are playing as oppressors in the first place. And while I recognize the horror inherent in the geopolitical sandbox of a game like *Twilight Struggle*, for my taste it is rather too muted, too easy to miss, or, for that matter, to dismiss.

I prefer my horror as text rather than subtext. Erin Escobedo’s *Meltwater: A Game of Tactical Starvation*[^4] operates on the same “internal logic” of the cold war that animates *Twilight Struggle*, but it more forcefully exposes the rot at its core. In *Twilight Struggle*, triggering a nuclear war loses you the game. “From our vantage point of hindsight,” Matthews and Gupta explain, “nuclear war was unthinkable, and that is why it did not happen. Yes, we came close, but we believe that rational actors would veer away from the button.”[^5] Escobedo’s game begins with the premise that nuclear war was quite “thinkable” – as evidenced by the decades of escalation and posturing – and, in fact, would eventually have been the logical consequence of runaway military budgets and unchecked nationalism. ^p57

*Meltwater* imagines a world that’s already been destroyed by nuclear war. The only inhabitable land mass is Antarctica, and there the survivors flee, only to find to their horror that the United States and the Soviet Union are still at war. Radiation slowly spreads from the edges of the continent inward, forcing them into closer and closer quarters.

Escobedo points out in her designer notes there are enough resources for everyone to live, if everyone living was the goal. But the goal is eradication. You can eliminate the enemy in kinetic battles, but you have very few military units – mostly, you control defenseless civilians – and such direct conflict, powered by nuclear weaponry, only spreads the poison in the air and the ice faster.

It’s more efficient to deny them food, water, and shelter, or to allow the radiation to kill them. *A Game of Tactical Starvation*. Through this, the game expresses the essential callousness of political superpowers. To this day, it would take a fraction of annual military budgets to end world hunger. “In a post-industrial world,” says Escobedo, “starvation is an act of either negligence or deliberate malice.”

It presents nationalism as a sort of cruel, meaningless joke: after all, there are no *nations* left. Even the distinction between “American” and “Soviet” is blurred, as civilians of each side can be induced to defect to the other. Neutral refugees arrive each turn, and can join your side or starve: they are just as much pawns as the countries of *Twilight Struggle*. The game gives us no borders, denies each side any essential cultural identity. People are people are people. Or, as Escobedo puts it, “death is death is death.”[^6] The game ends when only one side has pieces left, likely surrounded by radiation: “If your opponent has no units left on the board, you win immediately – even if your units would be doomed to death later in the same turn. With your last breath, spit on your enemy’s grave.”[^7]

Most matches will end in the manner described, the victor themselves just a turn or two from obliteration. These victory conditions reflect the “internal logic” of the cold war, and show it to be utterly illogical and inhumane. It’s a refutation of the idea of rational actors working within the system to narrowly avert the abyss; it’s the rational actors, after all, that allow hundreds of millions to starve. The system itself is rotten and must be torn down. The ideas behind it are poison and must be rejected. By showing us the perverse end result of nationalism, militarism, and superpower politics while constructing player actions and blinkered victory conditions aligned with those systems and their goals, *Meltwater* both immerses us and repels us, and through this tension makes a systemic critique. ^p58

This repulsion is key to Escobedo’s approach, a concept we’ll explore more fully in our next chapter. It’s immersion not as an act of empathy, but of horror. This is compounded by an air of inevitability. The game’s chess-like determinism creates dread on a mechanical level; often we can see the shape of the game in its earliest turns, and can predict its conclusion and victor well ahead of the final act. Those last turns are not thrilling, but resigned and disquieted. This allows us to recognize the insane immorality of the systems the game asks us to participate in.

## Ordinary Complicity and Fancy Hats

What it doesn’t get at, what it can’t really get at, is *why* people participate in those real-life systems in the first place. This is not a flaw in the game, but a matter of emphasis. In order to create its horror, the game is hyper-focused on how *wrong* this is. The game is shouting through furious tears: *Why can’t you see this?* It’s a call to action and to awareness. It cannot extend its sympathies to the rational people weighing the worth of human lives; that’s not the purpose of it.

Cole Wehrle’s *John Company* is a horror story told at the individual level. Like *Pax Pamir*, this was initially published through Phil Eklund’s company Sierra Madre Games before being published by Wehrle in a substantially different second edition. That edition lays out its thesis directly on the first page of the rulebook. Players “assume the roles of ambitious families attempting to use the British East India Company for personal gain.” He notes the “game isn’t about wealth; it’s about reputation” – securing luxurious estates, favorable marriages, and social connections.[^8] All these things earn victory points, which determine the game’s winner.

The game sees players operate the machinery of empire – conquering territory, putting down revolts, exploiting the people of India and its resources – but its emphasis is on social climbing and financial security in London. Wehrle pulls a similar trick in *An Infamous Traffic*, the game he designed between *Pax Pamir* and *John Company*, and which I had the pleasure to publish. There, the players operate British firms in the opium trade in China. But all your profits are funneled into the London Season, where you compete for a variety of prizes such as a mansion, a marriage, or a fancy hat. The hat comes up a lot in discussions of the game, and serves as a synecdoche for the game’s theme: all this suffering, all this ghoulish business, and it’s all for a fancy hat. ^p59

As I discussed previously, publishing *An Infamous Traffic* changed my life in a number of ways. And one of those is it allowed me to quit my day job to concentrate on weird board games full-time. At the time, I was working for a default management company. When a bank foreclosed on someone’s home, they hired us to protect their investment through a nationwide network of contractors. Changing the locks, cleaning mold, maintaining the yard, and so on. Eventually, the home would go back on the market, or be turned over to government-sponsored entities like Freddie Mac or Fannie Mae, which had secured the loan in the first place. This industry had kinda puttered about until the financial crisis in 2008, at which point there were suddenly millions of homes to be maintained. Companies like the one I worked for grew from a couple dozen employees to hundreds or thousands, and they were mostly looking for warm bodies to deal with the sudden explosion of their industry.

That’s what I was in 2012: a warm body. I had been stuck in a dead-end part-time civil service job where I was making less than ten dollars an hour, trying to keep current with payments on my own sub-prime mortgage. My spouse was unemployed, and had been sick with untreated hyperthyroidism for the entirety of our then eight-year marriage: we couldn’t afford insurance through my job, and even if we could, at the time she would have been denied care due to it being a “pre-existing condition” (the portion of the 2010 Affordable Care Act which prohibited such exclusions not becoming active until 2014). So when a friend heard this place was hiring, they passed it along to me, I applied, and I was hired.

I was intensely aware my ability to remain housed was contingent on others losing theirs. It was not work I believed in on a moral level. My political consciousness was not as developed as it is today, but if pressed I would have likely agreed I thought housing to be a human right. I certainly saw these people as victims, preyed on by a predatory industry.

This was not abstract for me, because I had likewise been a victim. I still remember the day in 2005 when the mortgage broker came over to our apartment to discuss the terms. He was the son of a coworker at my civil service job, and was only a year or two older than I was. He told us how underhanded the industry can be. ^p60

> Sometimes, they’ll tell you one number, and when you get to the signing, it’s not the same number. That’s why you need what’s called a good faith estimate. It has it in writing. If you don’t have that, and you show up to the signing, what choice do you have? Walk away? You lose your deposit that way. For a lot of folks, it’s their whole savings. A lot of times, the broker doesn’t even show up. Because he knows he’s fucked you over. But I’m not going to do that to you.

With the amount we were putting down – essentially, every penny we had – we qualified for a surprisingly low fixed interest rate. The broker promised he’d mail the good faith estimate the following week.

But it didn’t arrive. I called about it several times over the next two weeks, and even drove over to their office two days before closing to ask for a copy. But they couldn’t find it. “No worries,” he said. “I’ll be sure to bring it to the signing.” When we got there, it was just us, the realtors, and the sellers. No broker, but he had helpfully faxed the paperwork over, including the long-awaited good faith estimate. But it now quoted an interest rate several points higher, and adjustable. If we walked away, we’d leave our savings behind and have nothing to show for it. So, we signed the mortgage.

I was so livid. He told us exactly what he was going to do, and then he did it to us. When I saw his mother the next day at work, and she asked how the signing had gone, I blew up at her. *Did you know what your son did to me? What your son does to people for a living?* “No, he wouldn’t do that. There must be some misunderstanding. He has a wife and they’re about to have a kid, he’s just trying to get by like anybody else.” I dropped it, but I wanted to keep screaming at her. *There’s other ways to get by. People can’t treat people like this*.

And years later, profiting off of the misery he and his colleagues had caused, was I any better? I made decent money there. We paid off our credit card debt, paid down huge chunks of the principal on the mortgage, and finally got insurance. Mary’s overactive thyroid gland was removed. I started as a warm body but I quickly earned promotions.[^9] There was talk of me being moved into a directorship four years into my employment there, and I was quite eager for it. I didn’t much like the work we did, but as time went on, it became easier to do it, and on some level, to justify it. I was a company man. ^p61

I mean this literally. I certainly couldn’t have transitioned there if I had figured myself out then. I know that, because while I was a manager, one of my team members did come out as trans. It was announced via an email from Human Resources. It mentioned her name and pronouns, and for some reason assured everyone she wouldn’t be using the women’s bathrooms, but a special gender-neutral room anyone could use. (In practice, only she used it.)

There were problems almost immediately. People gawking or refusing to use her name. Her work credentials were all tied to her deadname, and the IT department claimed this was impossible to change in the computer system. I knew this wasn’t true, and kicked up a fuss about it. I also insisted my team members use her name and pronouns. I was called in to see my boss. I liked my boss. She had treated me well, taken an interest in my career there. And then this person I respected told me no one had to use my team member’s name until she had legally changed it, and that pronouns could only be insisted upon after “*he* gets the full sex change surgery.”

I tried to do what I could within this system, but it amounted to very little. About two weeks after coming out, she transferred to the night shift, a skeleton crew where she hoped there would be less harassment. A week or so after, she was fired for “performance issues” – issues which had not been apparent during her time on my team.

I would love to say this led me to quit in protest. Or one day, I saw a contractor’s photograph of a foreclosed home, something I had done thousands of times, but this time, it broke my heart. But I hung on, eyes still on the directorship they jangled in front of me. When the economy recovered, the company’s business dried up. Hundreds of people were fired, and my fellow managers openly prayed for another crash.

There were only a few hundred of us left when the company was radically restructured, removing the kind of middle management position I had been aiming for. My old boss had been a director for a decade, and was now the manager of my team. I was moved to another department. That’s what finally did it. Not moral outrage at how they had treated my team member, or the fact the business depended on millions of people being removed from their homes. It was because I could no longer attain the object of my ambitions that I wanted to leave. I began looking for other jobs. I still hadn’t found one by the time a day job was made unnecessary by *An Infamous Traffic*, a game in which players cause enormous, incomprehensible suffering in pursuit of a fancy hat. ^p62

And maybe I wasn’t aiming for a fancy hat – my passion for millinery didn’t really kick off until my transition – and maybe my ambitions were smaller than those of the families in *Traffic* and *John Company*. I didn’t want a mansion; I wanted to keep my house. I wanted to remove the goiter which had been crushing my wife’s windpipe to a narrow wheeze. I wanted to be free of the weight of poverty and failure and shame that had been bearing down on me my entire life: I wanted to *breathe*.

We all live in a system which demands money in order to survive and to thrive, and which creates barriers to make those things both difficult and compulsory. Most people must work for someone else, a system which is inherently exploitive and coercive; most work which an employer will pay us for causes some degree of harm. Even those of us who work for ourselves are not free of this. I make board games for a living, and I struggle with the ethicalness of making physical paper-based products on a planet in ecological crisis.

## Complicity in John Company

Wehrle frames *John Company* as “a game about the ordinary actors of empire and how often well-meaning and decent people can do some truly evil things to advance their own prospects.”[^10] Both Escobedo in *Meltwater* and Wehrle in *John Company* examine complicity through the framing of their victory conditions.

Their approaches and aims are quite different. Wehrle once said of historical games that the “proper POV for players and their victory conditions should be in alignment and free from the judgment of the present.”[^11] Escobedo is absolutely judging the entire rotten system and the people who prop it up, telling active participants in oppression to not play *Meltwater*: “Please grab your copy, take it outside to your backyard or balcony or whatever, and burn it. I can’t reach you. I really don’t want you playing my game about state violence with a fucking erection.”[^12] Wehrle’s game is an act of empathy and understanding, Escobedo’s of rage and anguish: both are compelling works of art precisely because of the ways their victory conditions and design decisions work together to express their distinct purposes.

Concomitant with this are the divergent approaches to agency. Like in *Pax Pamir*, the players in *John Company* have limited control over their circumstances. The outcome of most events depend on dice rolls, leaning heavily on output randomness.[^13] This even extends to the game’s “retirement” mechanism. This determines when players’ family members leave the company to buy the estates which yield victory points. You might be flush with cash this turn, but if you flub the retirement roll, it’s not going to do you much good. ^p63

Or maybe you’re saving up for something nice when suddenly one of your family members is forced out by the roll; now you’ll have to settle for something cheaper and less prestigious. This can all be mitigated, the odds of a dice roll shifted in your favor, but the primary means of control a player has is through negotiation with other players. Everything in the game can be traded freely, emphasizing the *social* nature of the game and its setting.

Again like *Pax Pamir*, players must find ways *around* a system built to limit their agency. Play is quite tactical, encouraging players to shift as needed and take advantage of opportunities as they arise. It’s a very human, textured story the game tells, with ups and downs and dramatic reversals of fortune. Alongside this are the terrible things the players are doing in faraway lands to faraway people which their characters’ unexamined racism and convenient dissociation don’t really see as people.

*Meltwater*, on the other hand, gives you almost absolute control over the game state. While the progression of radiation and the arrival of refugees is determined randomly, each card is revealed a full turn before it takes place, giving players perfect information on their turn. There are no dice here, no wild swings of fortune; it’s out of place with the game’s brutal arithmetic. By giving her players complete control over the atrocities they commit, laid out in clear chains of cause-and-effect, Escobedo denies them the opportunity to deny complicity. There’s nothing for you to hide behind.

Both games, then, depend on dissonance and tension between immersion and horror. In the case of *John Company*, it’s the tension between the players’ genteel ambitions and the brutal subjugation of India they participate in to achieve them. For *Meltwater*, it’s the tension between the game’s annihilationist goal and our recognition of its inherent insanity. This is something to keep in mind during our next chapter, in which we’ll examine the approach of alienation. At first blush, alienating one’s audience seems to be the opposite of immersing them, and for ease of explanation, I will often frame it this way. In actuality, all games examining real-life systems are both immersive and alienating, and it is this tension which creates a game’s particular resonances. ^p64

## Ethical Considerations of Immersion

Both of the games just discussed cast players in roles which are complicit in wrongdoing, and incentivize immoral and repugnant actions. As mentioned previously, this complicity is necessary to systemic critique; while it’s possible (and valuable!) to position players in identities in opposition to systems of oppression, in order to *understand* a system on a systemic level, someone will need to be pulling the levers. Both *Meltwater* and *John Company* likewise ask disquieting questions *about* personal complicity.

There are obvious pitfalls to this approach, however, and ethical considerations about which identities a game chooses to center. In 2019, GMT Games announced *Scramble for Africa*, which took as its subject the colonization, exploitation, and genocides perpetrated by Europeans in Africa during the nineteenth century. Players would take on the roles of these European powers. The game’s victory conditions were in alignment with those powers and their attitudes.

These attitudes were even reflected in the continent’s geography, which was randomized to create opportunities for exploring the “mysteries” of Darkest Africa. Alongside the game’s rather flippant tone, this drew widespread disapprobation, eventually leading to the game’s cancellation. It was questioned whether this topic was appropriate for a game at all, let alone a game in which the African perspective is absent, and the European perspective privileged.

*Scramble*, or at least the version of *Scramble* we can reconstruct through its tone-deaf advertising, did not engage seriously or thoughtfully with its subject matter. The game did not have any purpose except to recreate atrocities for some light giggles. I think this sets it apart from a serious game like *John Company*.[^14] While that game’s tensions function as anti-imperialist critique – tensions which, as far as anyone could tell, were absent from *Scramble* – *John Company* is also yet another game about imperialism from the imperialist’s point of view. The rulebook acknowledges this, and even cautions prospective players to “make sure everyone in your group consents to this exploration before playing.”[^15]

Is it enough? If a game engages with its subject thoughtfully, seriously, and with a purpose, is any identity off-limits? I am reminded of the 2002 film *Dahmer*. Anchored by a sensitive performance by Jeremy Renner as serial killer Jeffrey Dahmer, the film portrays his mental illness and anguish. Renner is all exposed nerves, everything hurting all the time. He is rendered particularly sympathetic in the flashback sequences about his troubled adolescence. ^p65

Rather than present him as some kind of unknowable evil to be dismissed, the film presents him as someone struggling with a sickness – a human being. A human being who killed at least fifteen people in horrifically violent ways, and engaged in necrophilia and cannibalism of their bodies. Everyone knows Dahmer’s name, but few can name those victims, or tell us much about them. Does understanding why he did these things help them in any way? Does it help us to center his humanity over theirs? No matter how sensitive and purposeful, isn’t this still just a reflection of our cultural obsession with the monstrous – isn’t this yet another movie asking us to identify with the killer?

I don’t have an answer for this. I’m not sure if anyone does. Art is a complicated and messy thing. It’s made by humans; how can it be anything else?

It can’t be neatly sorted into boxes labeled “moral” and “immoral”, “responsible” and “irresponsible” – often it is all these things at the same time – but neither can we use that to just throw up our hands or equivocate. I know I’ve no interest in playing *Scramble for Africa* should it ever see the light of day. I know I think *John Company* is a vitally important work of board game art which asks interesting questions about personal complicity within larger systems. But I also know I’m a white American operating from what Wehrle once called “the privilege of distance”; I would imagine others might have a much more complicated relationship with the game, if they have any interest in playing it at all.
