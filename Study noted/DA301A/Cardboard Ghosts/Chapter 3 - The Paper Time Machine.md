## History of Professional Wargaming

Games are an art form, but also a kind of technology – a scientific tool with practical applications. And like most technologies throughout most of human history, its advancement was driven by war.

The modern tradition of simulation games, or games-as-models, is inextricably intertwined with the wargame – a word that really refers to at least three distinct types of games. These three overlap and sometimes cross pollinate, but can be quite different in their purposes, traditions, and lexicons. My major focus here will be the commercial board wargame. We will also need to dip our toes in professional wargaming – a training and predictive tool used by the military and policy analysts. I will not however be discussing miniatures wargaming – itself divided in a historically-based hobby and a fantastical one, with those two halves united by the desperate hope you will one day finish painting all your little plastic dolls and get to actually play with them.

The use of games to model battlefield tactics and military strategy is likely as old as games themselves. Chess, the most obvious example, represents what in its earliest forms was recognizable as an army of soldiers, mounted knights, chariots, and war elephants. But chess is a poor simulation of what might actually occur in a battle. For example, the ground upon which a contest is fought is hugely important, but a checkerboard has no terrain features – no hills to offer a defensive position, no rivers to slow one’s advance.

With this and other factors in mind, the German mathematician Johann Christian Ludwig Hellwig published his *Kriegsspiel* – literally, “war game” – in 1780. This chess variant introduced river squares, mountain squares, and other terrain types using a massive board comprising over 1,600 squares – twenty-five times as large. It added pieces, such as artillery, to represent the armies of the time, and their movement capacities were set to roughly model those of real-life units. ^p21

It was quite popular, both with military officers and the general public, and inspired a number of other chess-variant wargames. These all however had fundamental problems owing to that very lineage. The discrete nature of chess squares and the rigid nature of grid movement did not reflect the sweeping of an army at one’s flank. Units were captured whole in a single move by the opponent, but in real battles divisions do not evaporate at first contact with the enemy. A modern model of war built on the bones of chess would always be a creaky, brittle thing; a new model could only be built from the ground-up.

Which brings us to the Prussian *Kriegsspiel* developed in the early nineteenth century: the one people mean when they say *Kriegsspiel*, the one that relegates Hellwig’s game and its descendants to, at best, a footnote.[^1] This work was begun by George Leopold von Reisswitz. His game had no grid, but rather sculpted terrain features upon which wooden blocks representing troop formations could maneuver.

Two decades later, his son, a junior artillery officer in the Prussian army, would develop it further. It was the younger Reisswitz who introduced partial casualties, and moved the game to a series of accurate topographical maps – allowing its use to make actual battle plans with disparities in army size and deployment. His *Kriegsspiel* added an umpire who would adjudicate rules ambiguities, and even control how much information each side had about the other’s position. The game won its thirty-year-old designer accolades and recognition, arousing jealously in the Prussian court. Intrigues led to his disgrace and exile two years later, causing the game’s adoption to be temporarily abandoned. Reisswitz fell into a deep depression and killed himself.

Over the next thirty years, enthusiasts iterated on the game privately. Among its fans was Von Moltke, the chief of staff of the Prussian Army who modernized its approach to logistics, communication, and tactics. Part of this approach was using a version of the *Kriegsspiel* to train officers during peacetime. Prussia’s dominating victories in 1866 and 1870 led other nations to adopt many of Von Moltke’s methods. This included wargaming, both as a training tool for officers, and to test the feasibility of various war plans. These games were often looser; rather than focus on rules and technicalities, the players would make decisions as they would in real situations, and the umpire or umpires would implement them on the board. ^p22

The human element is notable, especially when compared to the rules-heavy, mathematically-focused commercial board wargames we’ll be discussing shortly. Historically-speaking, we have many examples where the personalities, priorities, and ambitions of individual players, umpires, and high-level officials would color the results.

Leading up to its 1940 invasion of France, Germany gamed a number of battle plans. Influential senior staff officers felt that an invasion through the Ardennes would “not be possible”, arguing that tanks would get lost in the Ardennes

> if they left the roads, and they might have to do so, because the roads were bound to be mined or otherwise blocked. In any case, tanks filing through the forest would be easy targets for Allied bombers. And any tanks that reached the Meuse would find themselves in front of Allied fortifications, which they could not try to breach without support from infantry and artillery slowly coming up behind them.[^2]

But the officer playing the Allied commander, aware “French commanders at every level would require minute instructions that could be generated only slowly”, realized that strong play on his part “would have given an earlier Allied response to the Ardennes offensive, with the result that the game would have ended in a devastating defeat” for Germany – a result he believed did not comport with the state of French command and control. Instead, he allowed his forces to be cut in two, giving a “confident portrayal of an Allied commander in chief who could be fooled for a fairly long time.”[^3] This convinced his superiors that the Ardennes attack was viable.

Conversely, sometimes this human element also manifested itself when superiors refused to accept the results of the game. Throughout the sixties, the Pentagon ran a series of wargames modeling the war in Vietnam. Every game predicted disaster for the United States – communist victory or a bitter stalemate abroad, with conscription riots and unrest at home. But these “unpromising conclusions” were dismissed because “the president and his advisers were unwilling to risk either disengagement or escalation.”[^4] ^p23

You’ll note that these Pentagon games were concerned with strategic-level conflicts rather than the tactical minutia of battle, intended to shape and inform broader policy decisions. Today, professional wargaming as a technique is used to model business strategies, diplomatic maneuvers, responses to pandemics, and preparations for disaster relief. It can be tremendously important, useful work, when done well. As Peter P. Perla[^5] and ED McGrady note “When it works, wargaming can appear almost magical in its power to inform and instruct; when it doesn’t work, it can appear almost childish in its oversimplifications and abstractions.”[^6] They can be subject to the pitfalls of any predictive model, including blinkered presumptions, unexpected events, and personal biases. A major difference between the professional wargame and its commercial cousin, both of which broadly attempt to use games in a serious, simulationist way, is that professional wargames tend to concern themselves with the future, while commercial board wargames tend to concern themselves with the past.

## Avalon Hill and the Birth of Commercial Wargaming

The first three commercial board wargames were *Tactics* (1954), *Tactics II* (1958), and *Gettysburg* (1958), all designed and published by Charles S. Roberts. In 1948, the eighteen year old Roberts had joined the army, eager to be a career soldier. Shortly before being discharged in 1952,[^7] he designed the first *Tactics* to “learn the nuances of the Principles of War in a context that was less noisy.”[^8] It was a very simple game, played on a square grid, with two identical, generic post-WW2 armies. Roberts sold about two thousand copies shipped out of his garage, “and the effort either netted or lost thirty dollars.”[^9]

Deciding to make a more serious go of it, Roberts founded the Avalon Hill Game Company in 1958 with a slate of three games – the aforementioned *Tactics II* and *Gettysburg*, as well as a railroad game, *Dispatcher*. The revised *Tactics* introduced a Combat Results Table (CRT), a method for modeling how disparities in numerical strength or martial skill impact the results of a battle.

Each unit involved in a combat is assigned a Combat Factor. Each side sums the Combat Factors of its units, and then expresses that as a ratio: 4 attacking 2, for example, is 2:1. You find the 2:1 column on the table, and then you roll a die to find out what happens: perhaps the attacker is rebuffed, or the defender retreats or is eliminated. The better the odds, the better the possible results. For example, in *Tactics II*, fighting at even odds (1:1) will eliminate the defender on a roll of one, and eliminate the attacker on a roll of five or six, with marginal results assigned for rolls of two through four. But 6:1 odds will eliminate the defender on five of the six rolls, force a lengthy retreat on the remaining result, and offers no peril to the attacker. ^p24

The Combat Results Table would be carried forward to Roberts’s *Gettysburg*, and to subsequent board wargames published by Avalon Hill such as *D-Day* and *Waterloo*. In fact, it was the same exact CRT used in all of these games, with the same set of results assigned to the same columns. You might be wondering, “Aren’t there fundamental differences in the way battles and wars were fought in 1815, 1863, 1944, and 1958?” And, yes, yes there are! Many early wargames fail to be compelling or useful models because of this genericized approach. The first land-based[^10] Avalon Hill wargame to use a bespoke CRT was Jim Dunnigan’s *1914* (1968), which was appropriately attritional.

## Jim Dunnigan and the Paper Time Machine

Dunnigan is a central figure in the development of board wargames. In 1969, he founded SPI – Simulations Publications, Inc. – Avalon Hill’s first competitor. (Though “competitor” is perhaps overstating it; Dunnigan and his designers continued to design games for Avalon Hill, and signed over republication rights to popular SPI titles.) Avalon Hill published an average of one new wargame a year, and those games were on popular, reliable topics. SPI’s first year, they published ten.

Some of these were on expected subjects such Bastogne and Anzio. But also included in that first batch was *Up Against the Wall, Motherfucker!*, a treatment of the previous year’s protests at Columbia University. Originally published in the student newspaper The Columbia Daily Spectator – the twenty-five year old Dunnigan was attending at the time – SPI sold photocopies of it as it was getting off the ground.

SPI titles were not as polished or as well-developed as Avalon Hill’s, but they had the energy and immediacy of an underground press. This willingness to experiment was paired with Dunnigan’s aggressive data collection. In each monthly issue of *Strategy & Tactics*, SPI’s house organ magazine, Dunnigan included a questionnaire of “fifty or more questions” to “keep tabs on the demographics of our market as well as to test new product ideas and to measure the acceptance of existing games.”[^11] Game concepts which customers expressed an interest in would be greenlit,[^12] ensuring there would be an audience. This allowed SPI to publish over three hundred games throughout the seventies. ^p25

SPI’s approach to game design was also data-driven. For example, topic-specific Combat Results Tables were created based on analysis of casualties returns. Frontage and historical march rates determined the scale of a game and the rate at which units moved. Dubbing them a “paper time-machine”, Dunnigan described wargames as *conflict simulations*, attempting “to produce a playable but essentially mathematical model” of war, noting that “Wargames usually contain several (or many) models linked together in a system.”[^13]

## Mechanical Complexity in Wargames

This is true to a degree of all games: they are collections of models and mechanical metaphors linked together. In a modern hobbyist board game such as *Scholars of the South Tigris*, these features are brought together first and foremost to create a ludic experience. The game might be about the House of Wisdom translating, preserving, and building upon the works of antiquity, and its model simulates the relationships between different language families, but that is not a focus. Whereas in this wargaming tradition, the model is the *point* of the thing.

The name Simulations Publications, Inc. was chosen to signal this. Per Dunnigan,

> wargamers are not so much gamers as they are very curious history buffs. While many military history enthusiasts are content to read a book on battles and campaigns, watch a good film on war or wander through battlefields, a wargamer wants to measure and analyze things… We were stuck with the traditional term ‘wargame,’ but every one who created or used our ‘games’ knew better. We called them wargames, and let others call them wargames, an incorrect term for an activity that was incomprehensible enough as it was.[^14]

This distinction between game and simulation manifests itself in the *way* mechanisms are linked together. Modern games are often praised for their elegance – for mechanisms seamlessly folded into one another, a chain of snakes each eating their own tail. Simulationist games have a reputation for clunkiness and complication, for disparate systems “bolted on” to the whole like some kind of Frankenstein. ^p26

In an elegant game, each part of the whole must justify itself ludically; in a simulation, each “sub-system” justifies itself on the basis of what it represents. The poster child for this in the wargaming space is probably *Advanced Squad Leader*, whose 626-page rulebook includes rules modeling for example the lack of radios in French and early Russian tanks,[^15] men on skis moving downhill,[^16] and being temporarily blinded by the sun.[^17]

This isn’t to say all or even most wargames are as complicated as all that, or as their reputation. Dunnigan’s surveys indicated “the greater popularity of the simple over the complex”, especially for bringing in players new to wargames. But as players grew more experienced, so did their appetite for complex, detailed simulations.[^18] The market had grown steadily throughout the seventies. At the beginning of the decade, there were about 129,000 games sold each year; by the end, that number was over two million.

The early eighties saw a sharp decline, and by 1990 less than 500,000 wargames were sold annually.[^19] This is often attributed to two factors which drew the attention of both established wargamers, and the sorts of folks who would have gotten into the hobby, away from it. The first is tabletop role-playing games such as *Dungeons & Dragons*, which scratched some of the same ludic itches. Indeed, D&D’s designers, Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson, started as wargamers and wargame designers, and the game began as a supplement to *Chainmail*, a miniatures wargame co-created by Gygax. The second nail in the supposed coffin was the rise of personal computers and computer games. As we discussed in our previous chapter, the ability of games to administer and track elements in the background allowed for more detailed simulations without requiring the player to grapple with a huge rulebook of tiny text arrayed in three columns. This scratched the simulationist itch.

SPI would go out of business in 1982. Strapped for cash, they took out a loan from *Dungeons & Dragons* publisher TSR, who called in the loan two weeks later, seizing SPI’s assets: literally as well as figuratively, they were killed by the tabletop role-playing game. The shrinking market largely comprised experienced gamers with a greater desire for complexity and simulationist detail. Avalon Hill responded with games like the aforementioned *Advanced Squad Leader*, and by founding Victory Games.

This studio was made up of former SPI staffers and published extremely complex games such as *Vietnam* and *Pacific War*, with full campaign playing times of a hundred hours or more. Avalon Hill closed the studio in 1989, and itself was absorbed by Hasbro in 1998. ^p27

The nineties also saw the rise of comparatively simpler games with innovative mechanisms, such as the card-driven system created by Mark Herman for *We the People*, resulting in popular crossover hits like *Hannibal: Rome Versus Carthage* and sales juggernaut *Twilight Struggle*. In the last twenty years, mechanisms and concepts borrowed from modern eurogames have made wargames more sophisticated, elegant, and approachable. A good example is the COIN series of multiplayer asymmetric games initiated by Volko Ruhnke with *Andean Abyss*, games that have seen an influx of new players, many of whom weren’t even born yet when SPI shuttered its doors, and are eager to learn about and engage with historical models.
