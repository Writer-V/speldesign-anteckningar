## Systems and Synthesis
Games, like film, are a form of synthesis, comprising various disciplines that both individually and in concert create meaning. A film’s meaning is created by writing, cinematography, lighting, set design, acting, direction, scoring, and editing. Computer games create meaning through audio-visual presentation, user interface, dialogue, and code; board games do so through the physicality of the components, graphic design, illustration, and rules writing, among others. Common to both these types of games are mechanisms and the rules that govern their interaction: systems.

I’ve often heard folks argue, especially regarding board games, that those systems are what the game *really* is. Everything else is just spackled over the game in order to make it appealing. By this reckoning, Reiner Knizia’s landmark 1997 board game *Tigris and Euphrates* isn’t about city-states and empires rising up between the confluence of those two rivers; it’s about laying tiles to collect sets of colored cubes. That blue tile doesn’t represent a farm. Never mind that you can only place it near a river. That red tile doesn’t represent a temple. Never mind that leaders can only be placed next to red tiles because, as the rulebook explains in a wryly acerbic parenthetical, “Leaders and gods always hang around together.” Or, put another way, the game models the use of religion as a locus of legitimized political power.

I’m not arguing that Knizia is necessarily interested in modeling this in any kind of meaningful or in-depth way. It is barely there, a whisp of a thing, at best a pleasing bit of congruence between the presentation and the mechanisms to help you wrap your head around the seemingly unintuitive rule that blue leaders don’t go next to blue tiles, they go next to red ones. If it was possible to strip the game down to only its mechanisms, removing all the art and all the rules text and all the cultural associations we bring to it – a task that in and of itself is impossible to achieve in totality – those mechanisms would not be readily comprehensible. ^p8

As we consider games as mechanical systems, we must always be cognizant that those systems exist within the cultural object of the game as a whole and are expressed through it, just as our veins and arteries exist within our body to pump life and function into our organs, muscles, and skin. As with the heart, if that system is not operating correctly, then nothing else will. I think ultimately it is this undeniable centrality animating the argument that “only the system matters, and all else is cruft.”

## Little Sisters and Cellar Doors
At least, I’d like to think that. Less charitably, I think it’s about denying the legitimacy of games as a form capable of expression. This pawn isn’t a person, it is just a piece of wood. That person hasn’t been killed; the pawn is just being removed from the board. The creepy moppets of *BioShock* aren’t little girls, and I’m not choosing between saving them or harvesting them: I’m choosing between gaining a small amount of a resource, or gaining a larger one at the expense of a largely superfluous end game narrative.

Now, I might personally think *BioShock* is a rather sloppy and manipulative example of the ludicrous black-and-white morality we see in games. One could argue that its mechanical systems are poorly designed for the purpose of expressing its themes, or that those themes are rather muddled. You might not *care* about the little girl (and the game does not really incentivize you to do so), but that’s a different thing than rejecting the premise altogether – of refusing to see the little girl as a little girl.[^1]

Concomitant with this refusal is the assertion that mechanical systems can only make mechanical arguments and express mechanical things. It goes something like this. *BioShock* isn’t talking about whether or not it’s morally correct to murder small children, because games *aren’t capable* of weighing in on this apparently hot button issue in the first place. *Twilight Struggle* isn’t a model of cold war politics (or, more accurately, the mindsets and misconceptions animating the period), it’s an area control and hand management game. This chit in the Canada space doesn’t represent your influence any more than the space itself represents Canada. If you remove those six letters from the board, what about the space, *mechanically*, makes it Canada? If a game is capable of expressing non-mechanical things, then it must be able to do so *solely* through its mechanical systems. ^p9

To me, that’s a little like saying that a book cannot express an idea, because they are not expressed solely through the letters and sounds that make up those words. There exists the discipline of phoneaesthetics, the study of words not for their meaning, evolution, or cultural contexts, but for the way they sound: their beauty or their ugliness. A well-known example is “cellar door”, widely regarded as a particularly euphonous collection of sounds, to the point where J.R.R. Tolkien used it as a synecdoche for beautiful words in general, when he said that in Welsh “cellar doors are extraordinarily frequent.”[^2] But no one would argue that “cellar door” does not refer to a door leading into a cellar. When the time came, I chose for myself a first name with a rather pretty sound. But the name in this case means *me*. Phoneaesthetically, neither the words *cellar door* nor *Amabel* inherently possess the qualities of the things they describe. To demand they do so is absurd.

As is the idea that in order for a game’s system to really express or model something, it must do so in isolation from the rest of the game. Mechanisms are metaphors. Like a literary metaphor, they exist in the context of the work as a whole. The green light at the end of the dock – the bane of many an American high school student – represents Gatsby’s reaching and yearning, and ultimately his failure and death, not because green lights inherently possess ambition, but because of the context in which it exists within the work. And like a literary metaphor, a mechanism can represent more than one thing simultaneously, meanings blended into meanings.

## The Stones of Turncoats
For example, let’s take an extended look at Matilda Simonsson’s board game *Turncoats*. It depicts a three-way struggle for political power in medieval Sweden. This depiction is abstract: stones of three colors are placed, shifted, and removed from a simple cloth board that echoes the folksy feeling of traditional games like *Fox and Geese* or *Hnefatafl*. Unlike those traditional games, in which each player might embody one of the warring factions and guide its destiny, *Turncoats* positions players outside of them. We are influencers and schemers, seeking to end up in favor with whatever side comes into power.

Stones begin randomly dispersed on the board, depicting each faction’s initial disposition. A stone might represent soldiers stationed in an area, or it might embody a faction’s political influence. Each player begins the game with eight random stones hidden in their hand. This represents their initial standing with the three factions. At the end of the game, the faction that controls the most areas wins the political struggle, and the player who holds the most stones in that faction’s color wins the game. ^p10

Perhaps at the start of the game you have what is nearly an even split: three black, three red, two blue. Perhaps another player has four or five stones of a given color, indicating a stronger loyalty to that noble family. Of course, if there are a lot of, say, black stones in player hands, there are fewer on the board, making that family’s position tenuous. A player that starts with five black stones in hand might then be motivated to improve that position for them.

By spending a stone from their hand, they can add it to an area, increasing black’s power there. Or, they might place it on the board’s large flag space to move black stones from one area to an adjacent one – perhaps fleeing an area where it is outnumbered to an area where it is now doing the outnumbering. The large axe space initiates combat, removing stones of an opposing color. Perhaps our player will do all of these in three consecutive turns. But each time they do so, it is at the cost of a black stone – and suddenly instead of having five, they find themselves with only two. A fourth action allows you to draw a stone from the bag and then return a stone from your hand, but there is no way to gain more stones.

Two might be enough to win the game if black comes out ahead, except by doing all this they have signaled to their rivals in a rather obvious way that they’ve pinned all their hopes on black. They might as well be waving a big neon sign that says “I win if black wins”, motivating the other players to sabotage black’s position in favor of red.

Of course, our protagonist began the game with eight stones in hand, and five of them were black. What if the other three were red? What if, now reduced to a hand of two black and three red, they begin to trade black stones into the bag, fishing for more red they won’t have to spend, since the other players are strengthening red’s position for them by sinking the black position our clever little serpent built up specifically to put a target on its back?

On a mechanical level, *Turncoats* is a game in which each move both calcifies and weakens your position. The more you do, the less you are able to do. The more you throw your weight behind a faction, the less valuable you are to them. That seems at first to be counterintuitive. After all, if you are supplying troops to an army, nudging them into new territories, and weakening their enemies, it would naturally follow you would become more valuable to them, not less. This mechanism treated in isolation does not seem to comport with how we would logically assume a model of political loyalties should work. ^p11

But taken in the context of the game as a whole, the meaning is quite clear. The stones in your hand represent loyalty, yes, but they are also resources and capabilities: as you stretch yourself thinner, you become less useful to your political masters in the future. The stones represent a *declaration* of loyalty – which as we saw from our example of that five-three black-red split, might not be the same thing as “actual” loyalty (if such a thing even exists in the game’s duplicitous milieu). The stones represent a *message* to the other players: an invitation to collude, or a challenge to stop me. In this way, it evokes late night conspiratorial conferences, or perhaps meaningful glances secretly exchanged across the king’s table, each pair of eyes hoping the other interpreted it correctly, and hoping the other is dealing with them on the level.

Because it’s a game about three factions being nudged by multiple players, the final board state tends toward a stalemate, and is decided by tiebreakers, first in favor of the faction with the most stones on the axe (battle) space. Here the stones represent one’s belligerence and military reputation, temporarily cowing pretenders into submission. The second is the flag (marching) space; here the stones might represent the spread of one’s support among the populace. The nature of these tiebreakers, and the game’s reliance on them, hint that this conflict isn’t really “over”, but will flare up again when the time is right. Indeed, Simonsson pinpoints the game as taking place over the course of some two hundred years when some wealthy noble families “did wars with each other and took turns being kings.”[^3]

## The Hidden Models of Computer Games
The stones, then, represent many things over the course of the game, but all these things together represent a dangerous political instability – a system of power that tends to reignite conflicts and monarchial ambitions. Given the simplicity of the game, this representation is likewise quite simple and coarse. It does not simulate these medieval political intrigues in any kind of detail.

A computer game tackling similar themes might be able to track a player’s affinity and influence not only with a given noble house, but its specific members. Instead of reducing a player’s resources to single multi-use stones, a medieval strategy game might track several different avenues of influence, such as military recruiting, harvesting of natural resources, influence within given areas, control of specific roads, and scutage. ^p12

A major difference between board games and video games is that video games can model a greater number of things in a greater level of detail, and they can do all this without much if any administration by the player. The video game does all the bookkeeping, automatically tracking hundreds of variables. This level of detail would be incredibly cumbersome in a board game. A physical game like *Campaign for North Africa* – justly notorious for its tedious bookkeeping of each unit’s morale, cohesion, fuel, water,[^4] and ammunition, among other things, resulting in a single campaign’s playtime of sixty thousand hours! – is a *fraction* as complicated as an average computer strategy game.

Not only does a computer game not require all this busy work from a player, it also doesn’t require that the player *understand* all the work that is being done on their behalf. If you are playing the classic platformer *Super Mario Bros*., you do not, for example, need to know that it runs at sixty frames per second, or that the game measures time in increments of twenty-one frames – a *framerule*. You don’t need to know that the game only checks for level completion at the end of each of these framerules. You don’t need to know that Mario’s x and y coordinates are determined based on floating *subpixels* – that is, an integer representing one-sixteenth of a visual pixel – that factor into the game’s modeling of the plumber’s acceleration and inertia. Collision with a wall causes Mario to be pushed back, but which direction the game determines “back” to be, to the left or to the right, is dependent on his subpixels. Mario’s position also determines the destination of pipes and vines, as these are tied not to those specific objects, but to barriers Mario scrolls past. The player doesn’t see any of that, only the results.

But knowing about framerules and subpixels might help you become better at the game – or, at the very least, will help you become *faster*. Speedrunning of *Super Mario Bros*. – that is, attempting to complete the game in the fastest time possible – depends very much on the knowledge and manipulation of these aspects of the game. Runners will perform small single-frame movements and deliberate “bumps” against floating blocks to manipulate Mario’s subpixels, allowing him to clip through walls and access warps through the wrong pipes, often in the service of saving one or two framerules – of shaving off fractions of a second. Speedrunning, then, isn’t only about muscle memory or execution, isn’t just about being exceptionally good at a game; it’s about understanding the game’s systems on a deep level. ^p13

## System Knowledge is System Mastery
Generally, understanding how a video game works will increase how skillfully you interact with it. This isn’t limited to extreme cases like speedrunning a video game, exploiting loopholes in its collision detection, or spending seventeen hours manipulating the speed of a pendulum across parallel universes to complete a level without pressing the jump button.[^5]

Some games with high skill ceilings *require* this knowledge to become even slightly competitive. Fighting games[^6] like *Street Fighter* or *Tekken* have rosters with dozens of characters, each with their own attacks. For example, Ryu, a franchise mainstay for *Street Fighter*, has a couple dozen attacks to learn, while Tekken’s jaguar-masked wrestler King has one hundred ninety-four. You could certainly play these games without knowing the combination of button presses and timings required to pull them off, without even knowing that these attacks exist at all, but you would almost certainly be summarily trounced by an opponent who has learned them.

More than the inputs, each attack has a number of properties – whether or not it can be blocked, whether or not it knocks down or launches an opponent, if your character is invulnerable (“armored”) while performing it, how much damage it causes, if it is “confirmable” (meaning you can perform a safer part of the attack input and see if it will land *before* committing to the riskier part). Achieving this level of knowledge – knowing not just what the attacks are, but their basic advantages and disadvantages – gets you to the point where you start to understand the implications of your decisions, and are able to strategize.

Going deeper still, the curious player will seek to understand *frame data*, the mathematical heartbeat of a fighting game’s punchy universe. Any attack is composed of animation representing three states: the start-up, the active frames, and the recovery. In the start-up, you are winding back your fist. During a move’s active frames, you are throwing the punch; it is here that the attack can damage your target. Recovery is the act of pulling your fist back after the attack. If two players try to attack each other at the same time, the attack with the faster start-up – with fewer frames – will hit. ^p14

Being hit by an attack puts the recipient in a stun state for some number of frames, in which they cannot move themselves. Meanwhile, the attacker is performing their recovery animation, and if it finishes before the stun wears off, the attacker has *frame advantage* and might be able to attack again. If the attack misses (or “whiffs”), then the quick-witted opponent might hit them while they are still recovering, a situation referred to as a *punish*, which might impart that new attack with certain special properties.

Knowledge of frame data is recognized as being so central to competent play that modern fighting games track and present it to players through a suite of tutorials and tools. In order to be good at *Tekken 7*, a fast-paced action game in which a flying robot with chainsaw hands (Alisa Bosconovitch) can fight a panda that knows karate (Panda), one must first learn and then internalize that the former’s down-forward one is thirteen frames on start-up and a dangerous minus six on block but is plus five on hit and plus eight on counter-hit, while the latter’s version of the same input is a sluggish sixteen frames on start-up, minus eight on block, and only plus three on hit and counter-hit. The game’s developers know you *need* this information, so they document it as transparently as possible and encourage you to understand the game on a deep and detailed systemic level.

## Cognitive Loads
But “transparently as possible” for a video game is still fairly opaque. The information can be naturally difficult to internalize, and the sheer volume of information makes it harder to wrap one’s head around. This is a place where the added complexity afforded by video games, and the blackboxing of its inner workings, things often seen as advantages it has over board games, might become a liability. Because board games must be understood and operated solely by human brains, they must be designed with human cognitive loads in mind. Their inner workings must be simple enough to *be* understood. Further, because a human brain must be able enforce the rules of the system, the game *must* be understood before anything can happen.

A video game generally teaches you how to play it as you go along. Barring an opening cinematic, you can begin playing the game immediately. An action game will tell you how to jump, how to fight, and how to cast spells as you embark on your journey. A strategy game will inform you what a resource or unit does, or what the properties of a terrain feature are, when you hover your mouse over them. You learn by *doing*, immersed in the experience. ^p15

This again is often seen as a clear advantage over board games, where all of this learning is frontloaded. Before you can play the game, someone at the table must understand how all of its pieces fit together. In order to make meaningful decisions, you must understand their implications across the game’s various systems, and how those systems fold into each other.

Considered solely as a ludic experience, board games have a barrier to entry not shared by their more approachable electronic brethren. Video games are, arguably, a form of mass media, and their culture has largely penetrated public consciousness. Board games, though growing in popularity, fall far short of that, and they do so largely because of this innate inapproachability. “Let me teach you this new board game, it will only take about forty-five minutes” is sure to solicit groans at a holiday gathering from family members who would much rather play a “normal” game instead. Heck, I am a full-time professional board game designer, and sometimes I *still* grit my teeth when someone starts a forty-five minute teach.

If you want to play a board game, you have to read the rules or sit through the teach. It’s the cost for entry. But what happens if we look at it as a feature instead of a bug? Because games are systems, and because we must understand a board game’s system to some degree before we can operate it, board games teach us to think systemically: they teach us to draw connections between disparate parts, to visualize the flow of one thing into another, to grasp the system as a whole.

Both video games and board games can model systems, but with a board game, we can see the skeleton of the thing, we can grab and squeeze its organs, trace its capillaries. The act of playing a board game is an act of dissection. Such dissection is only possible in video games with a programmer’s coding knowledge, and further, a programmer’s self-directed determination to go beyond the intended play experience. But with board games, this act occurs naturally within play.

## Primitive Polemics
This makes the form not only well-suited for modeling systems, but for *understanding* them. Board games can be a phenomenal polemic and didactic tool. One of the earliest and most obvious examples of this is, of course, *The Landlord’s Game*. First published by Lizzie Magie in 1906, the ^p16

> object of the game is not only to afford amusement to the players, but to illustrate to them how under the present or prevailing system of land tenure, the landlord has an advantage over other enterprises and also how the single tax would discourage land speculation.[^7]

The rules include such barbed delights as “La Swelle Hotel”, which “represents the distinction made between classes”: a player with at least two thousand dollars may pay $25 to stay there, and a player with less “throws his dice again and moves backward”. When a player throws doubles, they roll again, but also roll a “chance cube” numbered in multiples of five. A roll of five means you are caught “robbing a hen-roost: go to jail.” A ten, on the other hand, means you are caught “robbing the public: take $200 from the board. The players will now call you Senator.”[^8]

A number of unauthorized and folk versions cropped up in the following three decades, and in that process, its purpose and bite were excised. A game which could be described as anti-capitalist was transformed into the capitalist fantasy *Monopoly. The Landlord’s Game* begins with each player being dealt eight property cards, mirroring the systemic inequality we are all born into, as well as the stagnant nature of property rights. *Monopoly* starts its players on equal footing, no properties in player hands but all those opportunities splayed out before them, reinforcing the Horatio Alger myth that anyone can make something of themselves with enough hard work and luck. Its credited designer, Charles Darrow, would become a millionaire as a result of the game’s success. Concurrent with the 1935 publication of *Monopoly* by Parker Brothers, they purchased Magie’s *The Landlord Game* patent for $500. Her game had previously been rejected by the firm for being too political.

Which provides an answer to the question, “if board games are particularly well-suited for modeling, critiquing, and understanding real world systems, why don’t they?” Most games don’t. This potential is largely unexplored. Publishers assume openly political games – as any game about systems and powers *must* be – will not find an audience. I’m not sure that’s true. ^p17

Marxist theorist Bertell Ollman’s 1978 *Class Struggle* sold over two hundred thousand copies for Avalon Hill, was translated and reprinted in Italian, German, and Spanish,[^9] and was acclaimed by the New York Times and Washington Post. Players are assigned a class to play based on the roll of a “genetic die”. Only the Capitalist player or Workers player may win the game, though “minor classes” such as Farmers and Students can share in victory by allying with the winning side. The game naturally gives the Capitalist player certain systemic advantages. For example, they go first, and they determine whether the turn order goes clockwise or counterclockwise. They possess a card allowing them to bribe union officials, preventing Workers and their allies from collecting the Assets they need to win the final confrontation – Revolution! And, if it looks like they might lose, they can trigger a Nuclear War, ensuring that no one wins.

This makes the game sound like it has a lot of strategy, but it is built over the chassis of a roll-and-move racing game: you roll dice, you move that number of spaces, you do whatever it says on that space. That bribery card is shuffled somewhere in the Capitalist player’s Chance deck (there is a separate Chance deck for the Workers[^10]). Assets, as well as subtractive Debits, are collected based on which spaces a player lands on. The Capitalist player triggers Nuclear War by happening to land on that space, just three away from the final Revolution space that decides the game, and the Workers player prevents war by landing on it first. As a game, it lacks meaningful agency; as a model of class struggle under capitalism, it’s barely there. Roll-and-move as a mechanism is not a compelling metaphor for what the game seeks to illustrate.

Or at least this is the case looking at the game more than forty years after it was published. Modern reviews of the game are quite unkind, but at the time, it was reasonably popular and celebrated. It had a reach that many modern hobbyist board games can only dream of. Like the best agitprop, it spoke plainly in the language of the masses: the language of *The Game of Life* and *Monopoly*, of roll-and-move and Chance cards, a framework most people would already be fluent in. Its subtle, gentle subversions of that lingua franca may have been enough to communicate its essential message, while more innovative mechanisms might have made the thing seem quite alien.

Certainly in my own work, when I make a serious “message” game critiquing and modeling systems, I am careful to work within the established, familiar mechanical language of modern hobbyist board games, saving more aggressively experimental mechanisms for other projects with other aims. If we are better able to model systems today – and I believe we are – it is because there have been tremendous strides made over the last few decades. Board games have a larger number of mechanisms to choose from – a greater number of metaphors, capable of representing a greater number of things in a greater number of ways. We have so many more colors to paint with, with a wider variety of brushes than was afforded to our predecessors. ^p18
