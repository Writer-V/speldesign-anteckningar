## Limits of Immersion
Immersion and identification bring players into a game, just as they bring an audience into other works of art. These techniques thrive on immediacy and verisimilitude. It is not uncommon to describe engaging with a film or book in terms of being “absorbed” or “lost” in it, which leads to a suspension of disbelief and grants us access to heightened emotional and aesthetic experiences.

When the immersion is broken – when we are reminded of a story’s constructed and artificial nature – it risks breaking that connection, taking us “out” of the work. In film, continuity editing is the dominant form precisely because it appears frictionless: establishing shots giving us a sense of time and place, medium-shots to bring us into the action, close-ups to subtly highlight things of importance, all stitched together seamlessly through match cuts and with respect to eyelines.

In every form, the most broadly popular works are immersive, because broadly popular works are, first and foremost, predicated on emotional response – humans are essentially emotional creatures. Works that do not connect to people on this level often fail to find an audience.

For example, George Lucas’s visionary science fiction film *THX-1138* depicts a joyless consumerist dystopia in featureless corridors and expressionless faces. It was, unsurprisingly, a massive flop. Marcia Lucas, at that time his wife, told Lucas it failed because it didn’t involve the audience emotionally. “Emotionally involving the audience is easy,” Lucas is reported to have snapped back. “Anyone can do it blindfolded, get a little kitten and have some guy wring its neck.”[^1] Lucas’s subsequent films have all been emotionally immersive crowd-pleasers; he’s been strangling kittens ever since.[^2] ^p69

## Too Close to the Gears
As discussed in the first chapter, humans are built for stories, and this is what makes it difficult for us to recognize and understand systems. Because of their systemic nature, and the systemic understanding required to engage with them, board games are uniquely suited to helping us understand systems, but immersion as a technique is uniquely suited to *prevent* that understanding.

Rather than let us see the shape of the structure, it invites us to walk inside it. Rather than let us question and critique the system, it asks us to imagine ourselves within it. This can still be useful, of course. Because immersion connects with us emotionally, it creates empathy for others; because identification gives us an imaginative space to inhabit, it allows us to ask questions of ourselves. But it doesn’t let us see how a system *works*. We’re too enmeshed within the gears to see how and why they turn.

This is especially true of asymmetric games, in which the victory conditions for a player, and the actions they can take to pursue them, are distinct enough as to provide equally distinct play experiences. My interactions with the game’s systems are limited to my part of the puzzle, and the things I learn about the subject are viewed through the lens of my faction’s point of view. Granted, I don’t want to overstate my case. Systemic insight is possible when observing how these factions interact, and awareness of how your rivals work and how they define success is a requirement for thoughtful play.

Volko Ruhnke and Brian Train’s *A Distant Plain*, for example, gives two allied factions – the US-led coalition and the local government – quite divergent victory conditions. The government is concerned with its legitimacy and its long-term future. The coalition has a more tactical view: how do we do enough so we can declare victory and go home, and how can we do it as cheaply as possible, minimizing our own casualties in what is becoming a very unpopular war? “They’re not fighting each other, but they have different views of where the fighting should end up,” Ruhnke explained on a panel. “They’re drawing from the same pool of resources and they’re fighting the same enemies, but you watch these players, and they are doing everything they possibly can to undermine one another.”[^3] From this interaction we can understand the dysfunctional course of that war, and might also reflect on the purpose and rationale for superpower military interventions. ^p70

My point, then, is not that identification and immersion necessarily always *prevent* systemic analysis and critique. Like any artistic pursuit, there are no rules or absolutes. But as a technique, immersion is often better-suited to engaging us emotionally rather than intellectually, and as any basic understanding of human psychology will tell us, our emotional responses often override our faculties of reason. This is at the heart of the spread of conspiracies and disinformation. As Jonathan Swift wrote in 1721

> reasoning will never make a man correct an ill opinion, which by reasoning he never acquired: for, in the course of things, men always grow vicious, before they become unbelievers… it is, almost in a literal sense, to fight with beasts.[^4]

This of course is another thing in immersion’s favor: if people are emotional animals, it is easiest and most fruitful to persuade them emotionally. But systems resist being understood emotionally; systemic thinking must be based on reason and logic. Board games themselves often function as a sort of logic puzzle, designed to engage with the mathematical and cerebral parts of our brain.

As I started to think seriously about using games as a tool to make arguments about systems, it seemed to me it would be best to lean into these qualities, and to minimize, avoid, or even work against emotional connection. If I wanted to make games which would foreground systemic understanding and encourage systemic thinking, I would need to reject immersion and identification in favor of new techniques – or, rather, I’d need to borrow a long-established one from other art forms.

There were two artists – one a playwright, the other a filmmaker – whose works informed my thinking and inspired my own. I’ll now be discussing both, and their ideas, in some detail before examining my own anti-immersive game, *This Guilty Land*.

## The Verfremdungseffekt
Bertolt Brecht (1898–1956) was one of the most influential, experimental, and prolific German playwrights of the Weimar era. From a young age he was deeply distrustful of authority and patriotism; as a teen, he was nearly expelled for an essay which argued, during the thick of the First World War, that it was foolish to die for one’s country. A socialist and Marxist, Brecht’s plays were openly political and didactic, arguing against capitalism, war, and fascism (he would flee Germany in 1933, and in exile wrote plays openly critical of the Nazi regime). ^p71

To make these arguments, he wrote, staged, and directed his productions

> in such a way that the audience was hindered from simply identifying itself with the characters in the play. Acceptance or rejection of their actions and utterances was meant to take place on a conscious plane, instead of, as hitherto, in the audience’s subconscious.[^5]

This is the crux of Brecht’s Verfremdungseffekt, often shorted to V-Effect, and usually translated into English as “alienation effect”, “distancing effect”, or “estrangement effect”. Through a variety of techniques, the audience is “alienated” from the action, and made consciously aware of the play and its artificiality. For example, the actor may break the fourth wall or express an awareness of the audience, who “can no longer have the illusion of being the unseen spectator at an event which is really taking place.”[^6]

Brecht’s scenes are frequently interrupted by songs.[^7] His intent is to

> set the music apart from the reality of the action… in order to give the right impression, that these were musical insertions, rather than to lead people to think quite mistakenly that the songs ‘sprang from the action.’ Those who object to this are quite simply opposed to anything intermittent, inorganic, pieced-together – this chiefly because they object to any shattering of illusion.[^8]

## The V-Effect in Mother Courage
To illustrate this repeated shattering of illusion, its purpose, and its limitations, let’s take a close look at Brecht’s masterpiece, *Mother Courage and Her Children*. Set during the Thirty Years War, it concerns “Mother Courage”, who follows armies on the march, selling them supplies from her wagon. Courage is a cynical, selfish figure who sees war not as a tragedy but as opportunity. When there are rumors the war will soon be ended, she becomes despondent, praying fiercely that the war will be prolonged. She is initially accompanied by her three children: ambitious Eilif, honest Swiss Cheese, and her mute daughter Kattrin.

Eilif most resembles his mother: a cynical, clever, and ruthless individual looking to war as an opportunity to thrive. He joins the army and is lauded as a hero when he murders peasants to steal their cattle, feeding his regiment. When he does the same during a temporary truce, he is arrested. Before his execution, he is asked how he could do such a thing, answering coolly: “It’s what I’ve been doing all along.” Told it was stupid, he retorts, “If I’d been stupid, I’d have starved.” Asked what they should tell his mother, he shrugs: “Tell her it wasn’t any different, tell her it was the same. Or don’t tell her anything.”[^9] They never do; at the end of the play, Mother Courage is certain her eldest son is still alive. ^p72

Swiss Cheese becomes a paymaster, which his mother hopes will keep him out of danger. But his regiment is overrun and he’s captured. Mother Courage has the opportunity to save him by mortgaging her wagon. She’s stubborn when it comes to the price, and instead of making a deal, allows her son to be executed. His body is brought to her later, but due to a series of circumstances, she can’t risk identifying herself as his mother. She pretends not to know him while the soldiers are commanded to “Pick him up. Throw him on the dump. Nobody knows him.”[^10]

Kattrin wishes desperately for the war to end so she can find a husband and be free of her mother’s control, and away from the dangerous warzone. With no end in sight, she considers prostitution but is prevented; continuing to follow the army, she is raped and disfigured. A camp cook inherits an inn, and offers to bring Mother Courage on as partner, away from the wagon and from the war. The only condition is that she leave Kattrin behind; he doesn’t want her disfigurement scaring customers. Rather than let Kattrin and the war go, Mother Courage refuses. As a result, Kattrin is killed by some soldiers when she warns a village of their arrival. At the end of the play, her mother pays some local peasants to tend to her burial, then harnesses herself to her wagon, chasing after a marching regiment. “I hope I can pull the wagon alone. I’ll manage, there isn’t much in it. I’ve got to get back in business.”[^11]

Brecht’s stated intent was to show war as “a continuation of business by other means, makes the human virtues fatal even to their possessors.”[^12] In service of this, he created in Courage a deeply unsympathetic character. She is unsentimental, calculating, selfish, and abrasive, “an active and voluntary participant in war”[^13] who is directly responsible for the fates of each of her children. Eilif, who from her learned to lack any conscience, is celebrated and then punished for his crimes. Swiss Cheese is executed because his mother is too invested in her bottom line. Kattrin is murdered because her mother cannot allow her to live her own life away from her control, and from the perils of war. ^p73

To further alienate the audience from this unsympathetic protagonist, each scene is preceded by a synopsis of its action, projected onto the stage. There is never any question of “what happens next”, and therefore no opportunity to get invested in the narrative. In the post-war production he describes in “The Mother Courage Model”, Brecht and his team used a revolving stage “to give the impression of travel”, behind which there was a series of screens. On these was projected, in large black letters, the location in which each scene took place, rather than use any naturalistic sets. “In this way we eliminated any vestige of ‘atmosphere’ that could easily have given the incidents a romantic tinge.”[^14]

Deprived of this, the audience could *think* about the play rather than *feel* it, reflecting on Brecht’s argument that war is business by other means, and that war, like business, *like capitalism*, warps human beings.

## Media Literacy
At least, that was the intent. To the playwright’s consternation, audiences and critics alike saw Mother Courage as a “helpless victim of the war” due to what he described as a

> deeply engrained (sic) habit [which] leads the theater-goer to pick out the more emotional utterances of the characters and overlook everything else. Like descriptions of landscapes in novels, references to business are received with boredom… And so, regardless of all our efforts to represent the war as an aggregate of business deals, the discussions showed time and again that people regarded it as a timeless abstraction.[^15]

At first it’s hard to see how people could utterly miss the point, and indeed, come to the opposite conclusion. It is not a subtle play, and it makes its points consistently and forcefully. However, there are two forces at work here.

First, it’s an innate resistance to ideas which challenge existing systems and structures. “War is business by other means” contradicts established cultural ideas about both war and business. When we hear it, we immediately, reflexively, come to war’s defense, looking for exceptions, for wars in which money has absolutely nothing to do with it. But none exist.

The Thirty Years War through which Mother Courage travels is, for example, typically framed as a wholly confessional conflict. It certainly was animated by confessional tensions between Protestants and Catholics, but those tensions came to the fore because of the church’s temporal influence and how it was tied up with the Hapsburg monarchy, how that monarchy related to the hundreds of polities which made up its piecemeal empire: ultimately, a question of power, which is itself always a question of economics. ^p74

Secondly, we have an inveterate need to identify with a story’s protagonist, no matter how heinous their actions. Consider, for example, the television show *Breaking Bad*. Protagonist Walter White is selfish, vindictive, and manipulative. He produces and sells meth, and the show does not hide how this drug harms its users. He murders not only his criminal rivals, but a young woman who threatens to reduce his control and influence over his business partner. For much of the series he hides all this from his wife, Skyler, who he assumes would disapprove of his behavior. He threatens her with his explosive temper, and in one scene attempts marital rape. The show is unequivocal that this man’s self-justifications are hollow, and his consistent self-mythologizing is a pathetic monomania. This emptiness is made extraordinarily clear in his well-known “I am the one who knocks” speech. But a sizable portion of the show’s fanbase bought into White’s self-perception, arguing in defense of his actions, expressing deep (and often misogynistic) hatred of Skyler, and putting “I am the one who knocks” on t-shirts: completely missing the point.

An audience’s desire to identify with and root for a protagonist is so strong that when confronted with something it can’t justify, it will lash out. Let’s look at another example from prestige TV, the fantasy series *Game of Thrones*. In its penultimate episode, Daenerys Targaryen burns the city of King’s Landing after it surrenders, killing thousands of civilians. Many critics and fans were shocked by her heel turn, arguing the show had previously always shown her to be a good and moral person. To their minds, this was a “bait-and-switch” and “character assassination.” I’m not really sure what show they were watching: Daenerys was averaging one atrocity per season.

But those atrocities were always happening to characters we weren’t incentivized to care about, in places far from the “main” action of Westeros, and they were being perpetrated by a major viewpoint character. *Game of Thrones*, and to a greater extent the book series on which it is based, makes an argument about the corrupting nature of power and the dangers of messianic hero worship, existing alongside a thorough critique of medieval systems of power and oppression, and therefore a critique of the fantasy genre’s unexamined acceptance of these systems. ^p75

No matter how didactic and obvious you make it, someone will always miss the point. Partially this is because when the point is the critique of systems, the prevailing culture conditions us to miss the point. And partially this is because when talking about narrative art, we are conditioned by the rules of narrative to connect emotionally rather than intellectually.

A handful of viewpoint characters aligns our sympathies. A three-act structure provides a familiar rhythm to lose ourselves in. Standardized techniques – the rules of “good” writing, “good” filmmaking, and so on – have been developed to maximize manipulation of our emotional response, and we naturally have become accustomed to them, to the point where their absence is unsettling and undesirable.

Board games, however, are not necessarily a narrative form, and many of the expected pleasures of narrative are conspicuously absent. It is naturally more didactic and more obvious than most art forms. When we want to use the form to tell a story, we have to work harder to make an emotional connection because the audience primarily engages with games in a cerebral, mathematical manner. But if we view this as an asset and lean into it, purposefully preventing immersion and identification through the Verfremdungseffekt, we can create a space in which players can think about a game’s argument.

## Nonhierarchical Art and the Monoform
This is especially true because turn-based games naturally allows us to create space and time for reflection. Most forms depend on what the filmmaker Peter Watkins calls a hierarchical relationship between the media and its audience, in which the latter passively accepts what it is shown, without being given space and time to think.

But board games are aggressively nonhierarchical. Unlike an audience for a film or play, players dictate the pace of the proceedings, pausing frequently while we wait for Marvin to finally finish with his turn – or, worse, waiting for Marvin to spend five minutes staring off into the middle distance before asking whose turn it is. Because of its interactive nature, we also decide where to look and when, which elements of a system to manipulate and how, and which aspects of an argument to engage with.

A concern with the methods by which governments and corporations manufacture consent and compliance animates Watkins’s films and his critical writings. Central to this is the idea of the Monoform, a “uniform and repetitive language form which frames almost the entire output of the [mass audiovisual media]” and “has been adopted by virtually all creators of commercial films, most documentary films, and by all aspects of television production including news broadcasting.” ^p76

In particular, this is a tendency toward “a rapid flow of changing images or scenes, constant camera movement, and dense layers of sound. A principal characteristic of the Monoform is its rapid, agitated editing.”[^16] This doesn’t allow time or space for the audience to question what it is being shown or told. Because the rhythm of the Monoform is the standard, our brains have become accustomed to it; implicitly, we trust information presented and structured in this way more than we do alternative rhythms.

Another element of the Monoform is way it impacts the overall length of a work, its pacing, and its narrative structure. Most films run between 90 and 150 minutes. Most network television shows are formatted to run for thirty minutes or an hour, with eight or sixteen minutes for commercials, respectively. This divides each half-hour into precisely-timed and often uniform segments. In a narrative work, these segments dictate the length of scenes and the pace of the action, each ending with a revelation, twist, or other plot point.

When it comes to a news program, each segment of airtime contains one or more news stories. What is communicated and what is omitted is determined by the available time as much as it is by what the news team wishes to communicate. And, because they are trained to think in terms of what can fit within a given increment of time, they are trained to frame their news stories in a way that will fit within that increment. And, in turn, we are trained to accept the news in this way – as a series of sound bytes and simple takeaways, without questioning what has been left out, regardless of the importance or complexity of the underlying story.

“The language of the Monoform mass media has itself neutralized our very awareness of its effects, deprioritizing or even blocking any attempt to challenge it,” says Watkins. He argues the

> aim of the [mass audiovisual media] is to create a non-stop stream of impact points… that will prevent viewers from being bored, and – crucially important – from experiencing a variety of reactions, let alone have time to reflect upon or query what is entering their subconscious.

This creates a hierarchical relationship in which the work communicates to the audience, but the word “communication” is “a misnomer to start with, since a genuine two-way communication does not exist”.[^17] ^p77

Of course, the ability of a film audience to participate in what’s being projected on the screen is quite limited. We cannot choose to follow background characters, cannot zoom in on details that spark our interest, cannot influence the flow of the narrative or ask our own questions of the protagonists. Alienation effects allow us to think about and argue with what has been presented to us. They create room for us to respond to the art, but the art does not respond to our response. The film, the play, the book: they remain immutable.

These forms are, inherently, hierarchical. The same, however, is not true of games. Video games, for example, often do allow us to choose where to look, who to follow, what to focus on. We have considerable control over the flow of time. We are as much the authors of the experience as the game’s creators, and actions we take within a game have an impact upon the game’s world.[^18]

I said as much to Watkins in a rather silly and impertinent email I sent him about fifteen years ago. Pointing out that many of the things he was working toward in film – often working against natural tendencies of the medium – were core features of video games, I asked if he had ever considered making a video game. Watkins’s response was polite and generous (more-so than I deserved), but came down to “I am seventy-three years old and unlikely to learn a new art form.”[^19]

## Alienation in Board Games
Both Brecht and Watkins worked actively and aggressively against established norms to deemphasize emotional connection and manipulation in favor of engaging their audience intellectually and making a critique of systems, cultural norms, and authority. Watkins achieved this by presenting his films as documentaries, “as though they are ‘happening’ and by building into this illusion a number of challenging elements, including ambiguities, which expose the constructed nature and fictional aspect of the films.”[^20]

Once I became convinced board games could be useful to making systemic critiques, I likewise became convinced they would need to include challenging elements and run counter to established norms. However, those norms are slightly different than in film or theater. Those are explicitly narrative forms, but board games are not. Narrative forms depend on a suspension of disbelief – that these are real people and real events we are somehow observing – but the inherently abstracted nature of board games already prevents the easy verisimilitude that Brecht and Watkins are resisting. ^p78

This abstract quality results in a different norm: that board games must function *as a game*, as a balanced competitive object, with each element of the game being constructed to this end. This is given primacy in the designer’s calculus: if given a choice between an element that is accurate but clumsy or imbalanced, and an element that is inaccurate but ludically sound, it is expected they will choose the latter every time. It is not terribly uncommon for games to be conceived as mechanical structures first, a skeleton in search of a suitable skin to stretch around its bones. Or, for that matter, to abandon its original body for another: the famous Castillo of *El Grande* was first conceived as the Trojan Horse, while the ancient animals of *SPQF* were transformed into neighborhood kids in *Fort*.

Even in historical games, where the skeleton and the skin cannot be so easily separated, but are deeply enmeshed, there is still an expectation it will function first as a game, and the definition of what a functioning game is has a narrow scope. The rules to a game must be unambiguous, rigid, and scientific. The game state is expected to be relatively stable and resilient. Players begin the game with a roughly equal chance of winning, and with many viable strategies through which they can achieve victory.

Victory is what it’s all about: it is a game’s primary source of meaning. Every action a player takes is in pursuit of winning. Each decision can be boiled down to arithmetic: move A is worth two points, move B is worth three, so obviously the optimal play is move B. The calculation is usually slightly more complicated than that. Perhaps move A is only worth two points now, but sets up move C, which might net you further points down the road. Because they are blunt and mathematical objects, board games encourage us to do the math, either consciously or instinctively.

Concomitant with the pursuit of victory in a stable, fair environment are the emotions aroused by competition. There is, of course, fiero – the triumphal rush of overcoming adversity. There is the sense of achievement we feel after building something, or the giddiness when our plan comes together, allowing us to celebrate our own cleverness. “How do I win” is a puzzle to solve, and moment-to-moment play is often about solving tiny self-created efficiency puzzles, resulting in constant hits of puzzle-solving dopamine: board games are usually built to make us feel smart and capable. All of these are positive, often exhilarating emotions. Even when a game frustrates us, it only does so temporarily, in the course of presenting a problem for us to solve, a process which generates eustress. ^p79

Modern games especially are careful to avoid mechanisms which make a player feel powerless, angry, or disengaged, such as randomly losing a turn. Partially, this is because of how far the art of board game design has advanced in recent years, and the emphasis on sophisticated games which reward strategic thinking and tactical acumen. But partially this is due to the centrality of victory conditions as a source of meaning, and the building of games primarily to support it. This conception of games results in a narrow but expressive emotional range designed to make players feel good, and with no room for negative emotions.

To apply the Verfremdungseffekt to board games, we therefore would not be calling attention to the artificiality or unreality of the game – games do this themselves by dint of being games – but would need to challenge the centrality of victory and its pursuit as the primary sources of meaning.

We need to explore a broader emotional range: deliberately irritating our players, frustrating them, making them feel clumsy and stupid, forcing them to wallow in failure and despair, even boring them. We need to seriously question what makes a game functional: do games need to be fair? Do they need to be stable? Do they all need to be elegant, each system justified and folding into each other like a kind of multi-headed multi-tailed ouroboros?

By running counter to these expectations, we push players away. Rather than being immersed in the game or engaged with it, they are forced to consider it from the outside. To ask, why is it like this? And to find that answer, they must think about the game systemically, and concurrently, think about what that system is modeling. In this way, we center observation of the model as the game’s primary source of meaning, and give the players space to think about systems. In order to achieve this, we often must work against the immersion techniques we discussed in our previous chapter.
