## Identities

The identity a game’s player assumes is fundamental. Commonly it is the first thing established. “You are a trader in the Mediterranean”; “the players control wealthy noble families in a dynastic struggle”; “you and your friends are a party of adventurers on a quest to save the world”.

This question, *who are you?*, is one a designer must answer before anything else, because it informs the tools the players have at their disposal, the type and scope of the interactions the game permits them, and the goals they pursue: it *defines* the game, and touches every part of it. If the question is not considered, or not considered thoughtfully, it can lead to an alienating incongruence between the player and their role – and thus the player and the game. If done intentionally and for a purpose, alienation can be a powerful tool, as we’ll discuss more fully in [Chapter 7](ch7.xhtml) when it is the result of an accident, it prevents the player from fully engaging with the game and with any argument or point it might be trying to make.

Naturally, games go to tremendous lengths to avoid this, immersing players in their roles and in the game’s world to bring these things into greater congruence. This highlights the two primary sources of potential incongruence: one, the players’ ability to assume their role and express it within the game’s systems, and two, the appropriateness of that role and its expression vis-à-vis those systems. If you imagine it in terms of a fancy party, the first concerns how your outfit fits you – size, shape, personal style: the cut. The second, how well-suited it is for the party itself: the dress code. ^p42

## Roles as Empty Avatars

Historically, many games – both analog and digital – help players assume their roles by getting the heck out of the way. In this conception, the role is not a character with an attendant history, personality, and set of attitudes, thoughts, and opinions, but an avatar the player can project these things into. This effect is more pronounced in action video games, in which a player often inhabits a second body controlled by their inputs. This area is actually a great example of the potential for immersion or incongruence.

In a game like *Super Mario Bros*., the controls are fluid, responsive, and tight. The character’s movements aren’t realistic – his vertical leap is about ten feet! – but they *feel* right and intuitive due to a reasonably sophisticated modeling of momentum, velocity, gravity, and collision. It is quite easy to step into this bouncy little man’s blood-soaked shoes.[^1] When he is underwater, his movements become languid and heavy: he *feels* like he’s swimming. No: *you* feel like you’re swimming, like you’re jumping, like you’re running under a fireball and darting between a rain of hammers. It’s immersive.

Compare this to the 1989 game *Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles*, a platformer developed for the same hardware. The controls are unresponsive and stiff. At the top of their jumps, the characters linger in the air a little too long: the gravity feels *off*.[^2] Its collision detection is unpredictable due to misaligned hitboxes.

All these problems become considerably more pronounced during the game’s infamous underwater level. For example, pressing the left or right buttons on the controller will make the turtle swim faster in that direction. Pressing down will make him swim down faster, a key interaction for navigating an underwater labyrinth in a limited amount of time. But the code for the down input is separated from the left/right code, and because it is embedded within the code for underwater currents acting upon the turtle, the down button *only* performs its intended function when there is a current present.[^3] Otherwise, the input is meaningless: the player presses the button in vain. The turtle performs an animation to indicate he is swimming down faster, but his downward velocity remains constant and slow. Even if you are not able to articulate the reason why, it feels wrong somehow. You’re not failing the mission; *he* is. “Why won’t he do what I tell him to do?”

You are Mario, but you are not the turtle. The turtle exists at a remove, a thing you control or influence but not a thing you *are*. Before my transition, I did not think of my body as me, but as a thing that contained me, in much the same way that my house contained me, but I was not my house. The disconnect between my brain – my self, my *me* – and my body meant everything felt wrong. My inputs were unresponsive; my buttons didn’t do what they were supposed to. I still performed the animations, the clumsy, laggy dance of masculinity expected of me, but underneath it, the code was doing nothing. Gender dysphoria is an essential incongruence between you and your body. Transition brought those two things not only into alignment, but made them the same thing: I am my body. ^p43

When designed well, the benefit of this kind of empty vessel player avatar is that anyone can step into it. Essentially, this is how symmetrical board games operate. The cattle barons of *Great Western Trail* and the settlers of *The Settlers of Catan* are simply seats at a table, allowing players to express themselves through the actions they take and the strategies they pursue – actions and strategies which are, at the outset, equally available to all players. We are equally incentivized to construct buildings in the early rounds of *Great Western Trail*, but by the game’s end, one player finds it both easier and more profitable to do this than their rivals. The player has expressed themselves through a “building strategy”, creating from the featureless clay an *identity* as a builder. At the heart of this genre of symmetrical strategy games originating in Europe is the quintessentially American fantasy of self-creation, of possibility unfettered by class, race, or gender. Within the language the game affords us, we can strive to become whatever we can imagine.

## Roles as Opportunities For Expression

Conversely, the roles in asymmetric game designs begin more defined, with a narrower set of personal incentives, abilities, and possibilities for expression. Yet, these identities are often more expressive and immersive than their freeform counterparts, because they give players constructed identities to inhabit and explore.

The best example of this in the digital realm is the fighting game, in which players choose their character from a large and colorful roster. These characters often have sharply distinct personalities, expressed both through mechanisms and audio-visual presentation. In the *Tekken* series, the jaguar-masked wrestler King is a master of grabs, holds, and throws – close-range attacks that circumvent blocking but can be “broken” by an opponent’s well-timed button press. His imposing musculature and highly damaging throws means other characters often want to keep out of reach. This large repertoire of pro wrestling moves is performed with showmanship and bravado, often playing to a non-existent crowd. ^p44

Compare this to the very different showmanship and bravado of Lei Wulong, a Hong Kong-based Interpol agent. Lei uses a number of frustrating and slippery stances to confound his opponents, including one where he lays on the ground, his head resting against a hand propped up by an elbow, throwing out kicks whenever someone approaches. He’s a character straight out of the martial arts comedy of Jackie Chan, laughing in the face of danger and refusing to take things seriously.

These two characters express different attitudes and encourage different approaches to play, allowing players to imagine themselves as powerful heroes or slapstick tricksters. Which isn’t to say this is simply a power fantasy. Each character has weaknesses which provide limits on what players can do. These constraints require and encourage creativity. For example, King, like most “grappler” characters, has a hard time “getting in” – closing the gap between himself and the opponent so he can execute one of his high-damage throws. This is especially true against characters with “keep-out” movesets, who specialize in maintaining distance. A King player must compensate for this deficit by finding creative solutions to this problem. Many attacks in *Tekken*’s three-dimensional playfield have “tracking” properties which adjust the attack to hit an opponent in motion. But Lei’s tracking is quite poor, putting him at a disadvantage against quick, mobile characters who can step around his blows. How each player uses a chosen character’s tools to overcome their deficits can result in recognizable and expressive playstyles. This can in turn allow players to explore, develop, and understand aspects of themselves. I’ve met a number of trans people for whom fighting games were the first to offer them the ability to play as a gender other than the one they were assigned at birth, to try on different expressions of femininity or masculinity in a safe way.

The capacity for this expression of assumed identities exists in board games, but often to a lesser degree. Given the differences inherent in the two forms, the identities we inhabit in board games are simpler and blunter on a mechanical level than in video games, and the mechanical aspects of those identities have far greater weight: there is no animation to imply body language, no voice acting to convey personality. It’s also worth noting that board games are always strategy games. They are played in our brains as much as on the table. When we inhabit a role in an action video game, we inhabit a digital body; when we inhabit a role in a board game, we do so at a cerebral remove. ^p45

All this makes immersion within a role more difficult in board games, though not impossible. A key factor is the range of expression afforded the player. Games in which the character’s mechanical identity is limited to a single power or cute little rules quirk are less likely to be immersive. For example, the Scientist in *Pandemic* cures diseases with four matching cards instead of five, and that’s it. I’ll use that power, and the group will build its strategy around it, but I’ll never feel like I *am* the Scientist.[^4]

Conversely, when I play as the Voidwarden in *Gloomhaven: Jaws of the Lion*, I have fewer hit points than my fellow adventurers and move slowly: I’m a vulnerable target who needs to keep her distance. I’m able to magically manipulate enemies into disadvantageous positions and move allies into advantageous ones, even giving them extra attacks: I’m distant from the action in some ways, but can bring out the strengths in my friends. The character’s fluff ties her to a spooky void dimension where she unwillingly spent time as a child, and so her actions often come at some kind of cost. For example, she can heal her allies, but when she does, they’re afflicted with poison, making them weaker to enemy attacks: when I help people, I also end up hurting them. The character’s mechanical identity is complex enough that I can roleplay as a deeply-traumatized woman trying to connect to those around me – not through improvised dialogue or character beats, but through the actions I take on my turn.

All these examples have been fantastical – what if you were a witch, what if you were part of a globe-trotting team of medical professionals, what if you took part in a deadly martial arts tournament, what if you were a ninja that lived in the sewer and ate pizza exclusively, what if you were a plumber? But the same principle holds when we shift our attention to more grounded and historical subjects. To feel immersed in those roles, we must be able to express it in mechanically congruous ways.

## Roles in Historical Games

Moreso than with fantasy, the question of the appropriateness of a role and its mechanical expression becomes subtler and trickier in historical games. It’s quite easy to know when a player’s role is incongruous in a fantasy setting. If the spellcasters and swordsmen of *Gloomhaven* were suddenly joined by a space marine who is so *guns* that his big future gun doesn’t fire bullets, it actually fires smaller guns and *those* guns fire bullets, it wouldn’t feel right.[^5] The purpose of a fantasy game like *Gloomhaven* is to allow players to imagine themselves as daring adventurers. Naturally, they will take on the role of adventurers. ^p46

The purpose of a historical game, however, is to model, explain, and understand a historical event, and so the question of who the players represent is not so obvious. What we call “history” is really a sort of farce, a complicated knot of coincidences, misunderstandings, personalities, systemic factors, and just plain dumb luck that we give a coherent shape after the fact. In the context of a game, we must not only determine which of these things “matter” for the purposes of building a useful model and constructing its levers, but also which people, historically, would have been pulling those levers, and ensuring those are in congruence.

This is often a question of scope and scale. For example, let’s presume we’re doing a game about the American Civil War. If we’re doing a game on one battle, the players are probably going to represent the commanders who led their respective forces, and are going to be moving pieces representing various formations under their command. Their decisions are going to be kinetically-focused.

If we’re doing a game on an entire theater, the players are going to represent commanders on that level. They’re not making decisions about how a battle plays out hour by hour; they’re more concerned with whether or not there’s a battle at all, the conditions of roads, and keeping their men supplied. These logistical considerations don’t really factor into whether or not this brigade takes that hill. If we’re zooming out to the whole war, we’re occupying the role of presidents and their cabinets. The levers we pull are going to be about funding the army, maintaining the public’s will to fight, assigning generals, and giving more general orders about the objectives of an offensive. At this level, we really shouldn’t be making decisions about which road to take through the Shenandoah Valley, where to place supply depots, or how to array your divisions in a battleline.

These distinctions sometimes become blurred, making the definition of a player’s role a bit squishier, or giving them access to levers they wouldn’t be pulling. This happens for two reasons: a desire for player agency and a desire for simulationist detail. A prime example is the civ-building genre of games, the most popular of which is *Sid Meier’s Civilization*.[^6] Over the course of thousands of years, you guide the destiny of your chosen civilization – you decide which technologies are discovered, what religion is adopted by your homogenous population, the size and nature of your army (and where and how they are deployed), what missions to give to spies, what monuments to construct and where cities are founded. ^p47

You’re not a political leader. The game’s scope far exceeds one person’s lifetime. You are some kind of god, or spirit of your nation, but one who carefully micromanages processes over which no man, god, spirit, or state could exercise control. Human invention is inherently disruptive and unexpected. Though religion historically has been imposed top-down upon a populace, and though a given religion’s place within structures of institutionalized secular power often gives it the same unexamined normativity as other modes of systemic oppression, people throughout history have rebelled against it. The game’s model gives you so many levers to pull! Each lever is a nest of systems and sub-systems, modeling each specific aspect in clever detail. But in doing so, *Civ* overwrites the wondrous and frustrating messiness of humans – the beating heart of the history it is sandboxing – making its model functionally worthless. I said there were two reasons for this, the level of control and the level of detail, but really, these are the same reason: greater control requires greater detail.

## Detail and Texture

The reverse is not *necessarily* true. It is possible to have greater detail but less control. In fact, you want a lot of detail because it is a fundamental ingredient of successful immersion. There’s a reason why period piece films put so much attention into costumes, linguistic idioms, set design, and even the lighting. Stanley Kubrick’s masterpiece *Barry Lyndon* is shot in delicate candlelight, captured with special high speed lenses, and it gives the proceedings a naturalistic beauty contrasting sharply with the garish technicolor illuminating most other films about wigs and waistcoats. It is perhaps the most immersive part of a film which often operates at a literary remove, complete with an omniscient and wry narrator, and the tension between these two elements is what creates its powerful emotional effect. Board games also depend on detail to bring players into the game.

Or, to be more precise, what they depend on is *texture*. Because it is not really about the number of levers to be pulled and the resultant complexity and fidelity of the simulation, but about feeling like the game’s world is real and lived-in. As we discussed earlier in this chapter, because board games are more cerebral than video games – more of the mind than of the body – players do not necessarily inhabit their roles as fully. It is more difficult to immerse them in an identity. But through texture we can immerse them in the setting and period. If their levers and degree of agency are appropriate for someone in that setting, they can imagine themselves living and acting within it, creating for themselves an appropriate identity. ^p48

I think this is the most important aspect of the *card-driven game*. This refers specifically to a style of wargame in which players have a hand of cards, which they play either to take standard actions (“operations”) or “for the event”, resolving the text on the card. These events comprise colorful historical figures, sudden catastrophes and windfalls, popular uprisings or swells of support – the texture of a time and place.

This tool was created by Mark Herman for his game *We the People*, using cards

> for army movement, political activity, and singular significant events… a hand of cards would offer a player a series of choices between using operations to move their armies or compete for the loyalty of the populace, as well as periodically allowing significant personages, via events, to weigh in for their cause.[^7]

For example, there’s a card in this American Revolutionary War game for the publication of Thomas Paine’s *Common Sense*, which grows support and political control for the patriot cause. Francis Marion, known popularly as “the swamp fox”, has a card representing his guerrilla activities in the south. This kind of action is far below the scope of a military commander conducting the entirety of the war, but its presence adds verisimilitude. Its mechanical expression does not strain the model or require a dense three-ring binder for a rulebook.

This is the beauty of the card-driven engine, and the reason why it’s become the dominant form for historical and political conflict games. One-off events are both part of a game’s system and apart from it, allowing the machine to run simply and elegantly. It lets you get at the messy human stuff without messy, picky rules.

## My Favorite Story

Though we shouldn’t oversell how messy or human these games get. *We the People* also has a card for Benedict Arnold, and it’s indicative of the game’s approach to these event cards vis-à-vis player agency and control over the game state, and the approach of card-driven games more generally. When played, “Benedict Arnold’s Treason Undermines the Patriot Cause” gives the British player an advantage in battle. In the game’s original version, this adds two “battle cards” to the player’s hand; in its later re-release and refinement as *Washington’s War*, which dispenses with the battle cards sub-system, it adds a favorable die roll modifier. In both cases, it removes Arnold, a strong American general, from the game. ^p49

And, this is fine. It’s a perfectly reasonable way to simulate this historical event, which had a minor effect on the overall course of the war. But it also presumes the event happens as it did historically: Arnold’s treason is revealed before he can turn over the vital Hudson River fort of West Point, of which he was the commander. British control of West Point would have dramatically altered the strategic situation. There’s a reason why they were willing to pay Arnold £20,000 for it – roughly the equivalent of seven million dollars today. If Arnold had pulled it off, a British victory in the American War for Independence is quite easy to imagine. The only reason why the plot was thwarted? A man was wearing an overcoat. This is one of my favorite stories.

On what I imagine to have been a crisp, pleasant September morning – being partial to autumn, I generally find September mornings to be pleasant – John Andre, a major in the British army, was on horseback. He shouldn’t have been.

Two days prior he had disembarked his ship to make contact with Arnold, and after the meeting was to return to it. In the interim the ship had been discovered and fired upon, leaving Andre no choice but to make the journey back overland, through enemy territory.

Disguised as a civilian, he had on his person notes in Arnold’s hand detailing the plans for the British capture of West Point. After a nerve-wracking day and a half of riding, he came across three men, one of whom wore the distinctive overcoat, green with red trim, worn by the Hessian mercenaries that fought alongside the British. Likely breathing a sigh of relief, Andre greeted his comrades and identified him as an officer.

But you know the plot was thwarted, so you also know how this story ends. These were neither British nor Hessian soldiers. They were American militiamen. The man in the overcoat was John Paulding, who had a sweetheart named Sarah. While visiting her, John was captured by her brother, a loyalist; John was arrested for treason and imprisoned. A few days before he met Andre, he escaped by leaping out a window and, while holding up at a friend’s livery stable, acquired the green overcoat. ^p50

Andre hastily tried to backpedal: actually, I’m an American officer, that was just a joke! He whipped out fake identity papers provided to him by Arnold. The men weren’t convinced and decided to search his person, upon which they found the plans.

Now, at this point, Andre might still be in the clear. There was a decent chance these men were illiterate. They would just scratch their head at these scribbles and take more interest in Andre’s handsome watch, which he offered to give them if they would let him go. But Paulding *could* read.

The plot was halted – Benedict Arnold narrowly escaped capture – and Andre was tried as a spy. This was the subject of some controversy at the time. Andre trying to flip Arnold wasn’t really spycraft, but an act of military deception. Having been caught, he should be treated as a prisoner of war, to be detained or exchanged but certainly not tried and executed as a spy. But because his ship had been fired upon, making return impossible, Andre had been behind enemy lines while in civilian dress: the very definition of a spy. He was convicted and sentenced to be hanged.

His commanding officer, Sir Henry Clinton, tried to intervene, opening negotiations with Washington. The continental commander offered to spare Andre and return him to Clinton in exchange for Arnold, now in British service. Clinton was likely tempted. Andre was by all accounts charming and capable, qualities which endeared him even to his captors. Arnold, on the other hand, was vain, bursting at the seams with bizarre resentments, and was generally argumentative. Clinton disliked him strongly. But the optics would be abysmal, and so no exchange was made.

Andre requested to be shot, an honorable death for an officer, but Washington refused. Nine days after he spotted that green overcoat, Andre approached the gallows with stoic dignity and placed the noose around his own neck. He was thirty.

He was dead because his hidden ship happened to be discovered, because a man knew how to read, because that man and his future brother-in-law were on opposite sides of a war, because there was a window to jump out of, and because someone had left a green overcoat in a stable. And because of that same flimsy pile of coincidences, the war proceeded the way it did. Change just one of those things, and suddenly the British are in control of a strategic fort. Instead of “Benedict Arnold’s Treason Undermines the Patriot Cause”, it’s “Benedict Arnold’s Betrayal of West Point Puts Patriots on Back Foot”. ^p51

*We the People* and *Washington’s War* don’t allow for this, and it’s easy to see why. No player wants to find out they’ve lost or won the game partway through because of a single card. Nor do you want to find yourself in a drastically different strategic situation, one which necessitates throwing out all your careful plans. To prevent this, the events in card-driven games are always manageable and mitigatable, privileging player agency and control. This is because a player’s role – as a head of state, faction leader, or military commander – requires it. History is rendered a thing you make happen, rather than a thing that happens to you. Never mind that John Andre would feel differently; no one’s playing *him*.
