# What Our Bodies Tell Us
A Story about the People from the North

By Caitlynn Aura Belle

**Number of players:** **1–5**

**Playing time:** **1 hour**

**Complexity:** 1

You’ll need: Skin-friendly paint, markers, and other art supplies—anything that can be used to draw on the body that easily washes off.

Goal: Players will practice telling a story both visually and verbally by using custom-drawn tattoos to accompany an on-going story about a mysterious culture.

Tags: Genre: Fantasy

Tone: Serious

Format: GMless

Content: Drawing on skin, physical contact

In this game, players will use art supplies to paint tattoos on each other, narrating what the tattoos mean in the culture of a fictional northern society. The story takes place over the course of one year, with each player speaking as a character and telling a brief story about the world for each month until the year concludes.

## Setup
To begin, sit together around a table or in a circle and have someone read the following aloud:

> *The Meskarr people live in the north where it snows year-round. They forage for seeds and winter herbs, hunt thick-hide game, and fish for strange creatures beneath the ice. They decorate themselves in heavy tattoos, which both tell stories about their lives and protect them from nature, by nature. We will follow them for one year as they add to their bodies, work their land, and demonstrate who they are.*

Next, have everyone stand up and reveal as much skin as they are comfortable with. Ensure everyone understands they will be drawing on themselves and each other. Have everyone state what parts of their bodies are off-limits and what they need to be comfortable with this experience.

Finally, go around the circle and ask each person to contribute two facts about the Meskarr people or the world they live in. Angle for a brief glimpse at a concrete, living setting. Think about how the people live, what they love, what they fear, and what brings them joy. Think about what makes them who they are.

## Gameplay
The game is divided into four turns, each turn corresponding to one season out of the year. During each season, each player will tell the others a short story about something that happens to the Meskarr people—either a specific person, a group of people, or the entire society as a whole. In subsequent seasons, you can decide to switch to other characters or groups of characters; you don’t have to continue with the same thread you started before. This game is about a snapshot of their lives, not necessarily a thorough narrative with a particular cast. Just pick what feels right and go.

As you are telling your story, you and the other players will begin to draw a tattoo on part of your body that symbolizes the story you’re telling this turn. Anyone can start drawing; anyone can add anything. The entire sequence is improvised. The tattoo can be an obvious form or something more abstract, but no single player gets to decide entirely what it is. Draw and add details until the story ends. When a player is finished for a turn, everyone should stop and admire what they’ve done.

Continue taking turns until everyone has gone. Note that the season has thus ended and a new one begins, and take turns all over again.

If you’re stuck for ideas for stories or characters, consider the following list of fiction prompts. Use, change, or disregard as suits your play:

- A fisherman who catches food for the hunting lodge with a ceremonial sword.
- The festival of growth, where trees are planted in meaningful formations on the outskirts of the village.
- A battle between sisters, near the shore, as ships approach.
- The priest and his chest of scrolls, on each one a dream of childbirth recorded, good omens.
- The dirt trails where fireflies gather and the love stories young genderless bards tell there.
- A storm that approaches but curiously moves around the village, never through.

## Ending the Game
The game ends once the fourth turn, Winter, leaves us and everyone has given their final story. Consider cleaning up together, or else go home and do it separately. The ink may vanish, but the stories will remain.
